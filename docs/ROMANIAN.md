# Romanian folk for trap: a working brief

For sessions that build on Romanian traditional music. Distilled from ethnomusicology sources and from how Subcarpați built their "underground folklore". A vocabulary, not a rulebook.

## The reference: Subcarpați
Bucharest hip-hop band founded in 2010 by MC Bean. They reinterpret Romanian folk motifs over hip-hop, dub, trip-hop, dubstep and electro, a style they named "underground folklore". The sound is melancholy folk songs and traditional instruments against dark, heavy beats: familiar and strange at once, folk warmth against sharp electronic edges. The folk material is treated as the foundation, never as decoration.

## Two layers of Romanian folk
- **Peasant music**: solo, archaic, pastoral. The **doina** is a free-rhythm, highly ornamented lament or longing song (rubato, melismatic, often over one held drone or chord), played on flutes (fluier, caval, tilincă) or sung. Its shape: a long held note, then a cascade of ornaments, then a fall.
- **Lăutărească music**: professional Roma lăutari in a **taraf** (band) led by a **primaș** (lead violinist): fiddle, cimbalom, cobza, nai, later accordion, double bass, taragot or clarinet. Virtuosic, ornamented, with harmony and improvisation.

## Instruments (and their engine names)
- Folk violin, the lead voice of Romanian music → `fiddle` (glides, grace notes, a gritty edge)
- Cimbalom (țambal), hammered dulcimer: rolling tremolo chords and fast runs → `cimbalom`
- Nai, the Romanian pan flute: breathy, sliding, plaintive → `nai`
- Caval and fluier, shepherd flutes: low and breathy → `ney` played low, or `nai` with more breath
- Cobza, a short lute that plays the rhythmic "țiitură" chords on the off-beats → `oud` with `bright` near 1
- Tobă and dobă (drums), plus the stomping of the dancers → kick, tom, `darbuka` doum, claps and stomps

## Rhythms
- **Hora**: circle dance, the symbol of community and unity. Moderate 2/4 or 4/4; slow hora often felt as 3+2 (5/8 lilt).
- **Sârba**: fast 2/4 with triplet melodies, giving a 6/8 or 12/8 feel. Vigorous stamping. At trap tempos, triplet hats and triplet runs on fiddle or cimbalom evoke it directly.
- **Brâul**: fast, tight footwork, often with jagged syncopation.
- **Geamparale** (Dobrogea): asymmetric **7/16** (2+2+3). Romanian musicologists call these uneven rhythms *aksak*. In a 4/4 trap grid, imply it with 2+2+3 accent groups or a 3-3-2 / 3-3-3-3-2-2 kick and percussion layout.
- Syncopation is the signature: jagged phrasing inside even meters.
- The cobza's **țiitură**: chords chopped on the off-beats (an oom-PA feel), which works like trap's off-beat hats or a reggae skank.

## Scales and modes
- **Romanian minor / Dorian #4** (1 2 b3 #4 5 6 b7), e.g. D E F G# A B C. The raised fourth gives the dark, aching, slightly oriental tension.
- **Harmonic minor** and modes with an **augmented second** (Muntenia and Oltenia show strong oriental chromaticism), e.g. D Eb F# G A Bb C (Phrygian dominant / "Hijaz").
- **Aeolian and Dorian** for the plainer pastoral and ballad colour.
- Doina-style ornaments: a held note, then turns, trills, grace notes and slides falling to the tonic. Fiddle and nai glide between notes.

## For the hustle (session 004)
The hustle: grinding, working hard to make it, underground, not happy but not defeated. Folk roots under a dark, gritty beat. Nearest wrong feelings: celebration and dance (the sârba's joy), nostalgia or pastoral longing (the doina's sorrow without the drive), rage (too hot), menace (too cold), tourist folklore.

## Anthem: "Deșteaptă-te, române!" (researched 2026-11-24 for session 010)
Romania's national anthem. What a producer needs to quote it with respect: where the melody comes from, what the law says, what it means to people, and what is known about the music. Not legal advice.

### Origin and status
- **Melody:** Anton Pann's song "Din sânul maicii mele" (on a poem by Grigore Alexandrescu), which Pann wrote down in psaltic notation and gave to the future Metropolitan Iosif Naniescu in September 1839; Gavriil Musicescu later held the manuscript and concluded (1895) that it is the anthem's source. Whether Pann composed it or wrote down a song he knew is open; an earlier folk origin is possible (Vasile 2019; Newsweek România). Gheorghe Ucenescu, a church singer of Șcheii Brașovului, brought the tune to Andrei Mureșanu (Wikipedia; Vasile 2019).
- **Text:** Andrei Mureșanu's poem "Un răsunet" ("An Echo"), published 21 June 1848; first sung in Brașov in late June 1848, it became the revolution's anthem at once (Wikipedia; Newsweek România). Eleven stanzas; officially stanzas 1, 2, 4 and 11 are sung (Law 75/1994 annex; Puterea 2024).
- **Public domain:** yes for the melody and the text. Romanian copyright lasts 70 years after the author's death (Wikipedia, "Copyright law of Romania", Law 8/1996); Pann died in November 1854 (Newsweek România) and the text dates from 1848. Later harmonisations and orchestrations (Musicescu 1889, Kiriac 1904/1909, Vidu 1934 and the versions played today) are separate works: use the bare melody and write your own harmony. Moral rights (attribution, integrity) are unlimited in time under the same law: credit the melody to Anton Pann and don't present a deformation as the anthem itself.

### What Romanian law says (Law 75/1994 and its norms)
- **Law 75/1994** (Monitorul Oficial no. 237, 26 August 1994): the anthem is "Deșteaptă-te, române!" in its annex (art. 9); it is sung and officially published only in Romanian (art. 13); "Cetățenii sunt datori să manifeste respect față de drapelul și imnul național al României și să nu comită nici un act prin care s-ar aduce ofensă acestora" (art. 20: citizens must respect the anthem and commit no act that offends it); the audience stands at ceremonies (art. 21). A note after annex 3: "Armonizările și aranjamentele muzicale ale imnului național se vor face cu aprobarea Ministerului Culturii" (harmonisations and arrangements of the anthem need the Ministry of Culture's approval). Amendments: Law 176/2023 (during the anthem the audience sings or stays silent; nothing may be added to the flag) and Law 20/2024 (organisers print or display the four official stanzas) (e-juridic; Puterea).
- **The norms (HG 1157/2001), art. 24(1)(i):** a contravention is "intonarea imnului național cu alt text și cu altă partitură decât cele stabilite de lege" (intoning the anthem with a text or score other than the one set by law), fined 2,500-5,000 lei today (25-50 million old lei). It was applied in 2011, when singer Marcel Pavel and the football federation were fined 5,000 lei each for swapping two lines of the text before a match (Știrile ProTV). The norms also repeat the duty of respect (art. 22) without a fine attached to it.
- **Criminal law:** the 1973 Criminal Code punished offending national symbols; the current code dropped it. Bills to bring it back (6 months to 3 years, including singing the anthem "with a different text or music") were proposed and opposed by APADOR-CH as a breach of free expression (ECHR art. 10); none is in force. APADOR describes offending the symbols as an administrative offence (500-5,000 lei) (APADOR-CH). A 2023 law fines adding symbols to the flag (10,000-20,000 lei); it does not touch the anthem (G4Media).
- **What this means for a track (our reading, not legal advice):** the rules govern *intoning the anthem*: performances of the anthem as the anthem, at ceremonies and events. A track that quotes the melody inside an original composition is not an intonation of the anthem, but the duty of respect (art. 20) and the ban on intoning it with another text or score set the spirit. So: no new words on the anthem's melody; never sing the anthem's own words over an altered melody (if words are sung at all, they are the official Romanian text on the clean, correct melody); quote fragments, not a full "performance" of the anthem; any clean quote follows the score exactly; the damage the track does to the melody must read as something done *to* the country by corruption, never as mockery of the anthem; credit the melody to Anton Pann. Releasing an arrangement *of the anthem* would need the Ministry's approval; an original track that quotes it is not presented as one.

### The music (what is sourced)
- **Mode and key:** minor, described from the start as "plaintive" (Adamescu 1898, in Vasile 2019); critics have asked "why minor?" (Chelaru 2018). Kiriac's 1909 school version is in **G minor** (Vasile 2019); the U.S. Navy Band recording is in **F minor** (Wikipedia).
- **Opening:** Kiriac's version starts with an **upbeat leap of a fourth** ("anacruză de cvartă") (Vasile 2019); in G minor that is most likely D up to G, the tonic: verify on a score.
- **Rhythm:** Pann's original is ternary, waltz-like (Chelaru 2018); Musicescu's version oscillates between ternary and binary, with half-beat upbeats at each line; Kiriac kept only on-beat upbeats and made it more energetic (Vasile 2019). The march-like version became the Regat's; Transylvania kept a slower, lullaby-like one (Newsweek România). Today's anthem is a binary (duple) march.
- **Form:** three 10-bar periods, a b b (b repeated); a = 2 + 4 + 4 bars: an opening, a climax (bars 3-6), a release (bars 7-10); b = a climax, its emphasis, an epilogue (Chelaru 2018).
- **Note names are not transcribed here.** No readable score could be fetched in this research (the free score at cantorion.org, no. 3849, Voice and Piano, CC BY-SA; ro.wikipedia; nationalanthems.info: all blocked by the network here, and the law's online text carries the words only). Don't write the melody from memory: transcribe it from a score you can read (the annex of Law 75/1994 as printed in Monitorul Oficial no. 237/1994, a school songbook, or a public-domain or CC score), note the source, and check it against the facts above (minor key, fourth upbeat into the first phrase, a b b in 10-bar periods).

### Cultural weight
- **Born in a revolution, sung in every one since:** the anthem of 1848; banned in Austro-Hungarian Transylvania (in 1890 Musicescu's choir toured there singing the melody **without words** to get past the censors; before WWI gendarmes stopped students singing it at Mureșanu's monument in Brașov); outlawed in 1947; sung in the 1987 Brașov workers' revolt and the 1989 revolution; adopted as the national anthem on 24 January 1990; also Moldova's anthem 1991-1994 (Vasile 2019; Wikipedia). The Romanian Academy's president calls it the only Romanian anthem chosen "from below" (Vasile 2019).
- **The anthem against corruption:** in February 2017, against a government emergency ordinance that partly decriminalised abuse of office, 120,000-150,000 people filled Piața Victoriei and 250,000-300,000 protested across Romania (Romania Insider). The crowd stood in silence, then sang the anthem together, "the country's largest choir", with drums and bells (Observator). After the Colectiv nightclub fire (October 2015), protesters marched under "Corupția ucide" ("Corruption kills"), blaming officials who licensed the club without a fire permit; the government resigned on 4 November 2015 (Wikipedia, "2015 Romanian protests"). In Romania the anthem is something people sing *at* corruption, not something corruption owns.
- **Romanian hip hop as a protest voice:** B.U.G. Mafia's "Românește" attacks corrupt politicians stealing public money; 1-Q Sappro with Moromeții's "România, trezește-te!" (2004) echoes the anthem's title in a protest song against the political class; Paraziții campaigned against censorship (Adevărul 2012). No source found of a Romanian rap track built on the anthem's melody; the closest use is its title.
