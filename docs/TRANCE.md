# Trance for Spectre: a working brief

For sessions that make trance (uplifting, progressive, euphoric). A vocabulary, not a rulebook. Spectre has so far made only trap-family beats: trance is a different body. It runs on a straight four-on-the-floor pulse, the bass lives *between* the kicks, and the emotion comes from long-form tension and release instead of a loop.

## The tradition and the references
- Trance grew out of early-1990s European club music (Germany above all), adding melody, harmony, emotional highs and lows, long tracks and prominent breakdowns to house. Influences: UK progressive house, Goa, sci-fi soundtracks, Moroder/Jarre synth music, Italo-disco. It was dance music's dominant genre in the late 1990s, burned out in the early 2000s, fed the 2010s EDM boom and is having a UK resurgence now.
- **Uplifting trance** (also "epic" or "euphoric") got its name around 1996, after the "rush" listeners describe. It is happier than Goa and darker trance, rests on longer chord progressions, and draws on classical and film music.
- **Classic references:** Paul van Dyk, Ferry Corsten (System F), Tiësto, Armin van Buuren, Robert Miles, ATB, Paul Oakenfold, Sasha & Digweed.
- **Modern references:** Above & Beyond, Aly & Fila, Gareth Emery, Bryan Kearney, Sean Tyas, John O'Callaghan, Andy Blueman, Super8 & Tab; the UK resurgence: Ben Hemsley, Hannah Laing, Ewan McVicar.
- Trance is mostly instrumental, but how much depends on the scene: the mainstream end of the genre is now mostly vocal, underground uplifting mostly instrumental. See "Vocals" below.

## Tempo and groove (engine names in brackets)
- **Tempo:** 136-140 bpm is the core (uplifting 138-142, vocal 136-140, progressive 128-134, tech 140-145; hard trance 148+ is a different, harder body). Straight 16ths: `"swing": 0.5`, `humanize_ms` 0-1 on the drums.
- **Kick on every beat**, short and punchy (`clubkick`). Big reverbed kicks are for sparse moments only.
- **Clap or snare on 2 and 4** (`handclap`, `snare`), with a 0.5-1.5 s reverb tail. Level, layering and reverb: see "Claps in trance" below.
- **Open hat on the offbeat 8ths** (`openhat`, `"..x...x...x...x."`), 16th closed hats or shakers for drive (`hat`). In builds, add 16th hats.
- Keep percussion simple; strip the beat back where bass and lead are busy.

## Bass: offbeat and rolling
- **Offbeat bass** (unsourced pattern, craft knowledge): one note on the "and" between kicks, `"..x...x...x...x."`. The classic progressive and uplifting pulse.
- **Rolling bass** (unsourced pattern, craft knowledge): three 16ths between kicks, `".xxx.xxx.xxx.xxx"` (kick-bass-bass-bass). Drives harder; it suits drops and tech-leaning tracks. Reggae-style rolling lines are a variation.
- Low-pass the bass (about 500 Hz) so it doesn't clash with the kick's mids; boost 60-80 Hz, cut around 200 Hz. A sine sub with short attack and release reinforces it.
- **Sidechain** the bass, chords and pads to the kick: attack about 1 ms, release 80-120 ms. The pump is part of the sound, not just a mix fix.
- Engine: `trancebass` with chord stabs and the `root` voicing, e.g. `{"chords": "Am:16 F:16 C:16 G:16", "style": "stabs", "rhythm": ".xxx.xxx.xxx.xxx", "voicing": "root", "register": "A1", "stab_len": 1}`, plus `"sidechain": {"source": "kick", "depth": 0.85, "release_ms": 90}`.

## Leads, chords, plucks, gates, pads
- **Supersaw:** Roland's JP-8000 (1996) supersaw, seven detuned saws, became the genre-defining trance sound. In anthem trance a supersaw lead carries the hook. Use about 7 voices and 15-25 cents of detune (under 10 sounds thin, over 30 out of tune), high-pass chords around 200 Hz, and keep them wide. Engine: `supersaw` (stereo; uses the JP-8000's uneven detune pattern).
- **Plucks and arps:** a pluck has 0 attack and 100-150 ms decay with no sustain. Arps run 16ths through each chord for 2-4 bars. Engine: `supersaw` with `sustain_level` 0, short `decay`, `env` and `fdecay`, played as a chord `arp`.
- **Trance gate:** sustained pads or chords chopped into a rhythmic 16th pattern. Longer release means a smoother, more organic gate; delay and reverb suit gated parts. Engine: `gate` fx with a step pattern.
- **Pads:** attack 0.5-2 s, release 2-4 s; boost gently somewhere in 200 Hz-1 kHz. Strings, choirs and voices are the classic "wash" layer. Engine: `supersaw` with long attack, `pad`, `strings`, `choir`.
- **Piano** riffs, with delay and reverb, are classic breakdown material (`piano`).

## Harmony
- **Minor keys,** most often A minor, then D, F, G, B and E minor.
- **Progressions:** i-VI-III-VII (Am-F-C-G) is the uplifting workhorse. Others: i-VI-i-V with a major V (Am-F-Am-E) for the emotional cadence, i-iv-VII-III (Am-Dm-G-C), and VI-III-VII-i (F-C-G-Am) for the breakdown uplift.
- **Lift:** major chords carry the joy, and the balance of major and minor sets how happy or sad a progression feels. Suspended chords resolving (Esus4 to E, sus2 colour on the VI) give the yearning-then-arriving feeling. Changing key by 3 or 5 semitones after a long build gives a new direction.
- **Complexity sweet spot:** in a study where fans rated generated uplifting-trance progressions, enjoyment rose with harmonic complexity up to a point, then fell. Very simple progressions were disliked; "awkward" changes kill the dancefloor. Trance clichés are part of its fabric: predictability lets listeners get lost.

## Arrangement and the radio edit
- Extended (club) mixes run 6-8 minutes with 16-32-bar intros and outros for DJs; radio edits about 3:30 (measured; see "Radio edit" below for their form). A typical extended layout: intro 16-32, theme 16-32, build 8-16, first drop 16-32, breakdown 32-64, build 8-16, main drop 32-64, outro 16-32 ([BeatKey](https://beatkey.pages.dev/how-to-make-trance-music)).
- **Spectre's extended shape** (6:00-8:00; at 138 bpm a bar is 1.74 s, so 232 bars is about 6:43): DJ intro 32 (kick, bass, hats; a filtered hint of the theme in the second 16), groove and theme 32, build 8, first drop 32, **breakdown 32** (no kick: pads, piano or the vocal-like lead, the theme stated slowly), **build 16** (snare roll 4ths, 8ths, 16ths, then 32nds; riser; high-pass sweep on the music; pitch rise; one beat of silence), **main drop 48** (full lead and chords, everything back, a second wave that goes further than the first), DJ outro 32 (back to drums and bass). Structure detail of the build is craft, unsourced.
- Tease the breakdown: strip to strings, effects and the "vocal", then bring parts back every couple of bars. Introduce new layers with an incidental (offbeat snare, reverse crash, filter sweep). Work in 16-bar blocks.

## Radio edit (researched 2026-11-24; engine: `--genre trance_radio`)
A radio edit is the short cut of a trance single: the same track without the DJ tools, made for radio, streaming playlists and listeners rather than for mixing. Spectre makes one only when the brief asks for it (session 013 is the first).

**Measured: how long trance radio edits are.** Spectre's own count on MusicBrainz (2026-11-24): every recording tagged `trance` with "radio edit" in its title (search split by duration to get past the 500-result cap), grouped by artist and title so a track that appears on many compilations counts once, length = the group's median. Guides that say "about three minutes" ([DJcity](https://djcity.com.au/blog/radio-edit-vs-original-vs-extended-versions-of-music-tracks/); "anything between 2:45 and 3:30 works", [Inspired by Beatz](https://www.inspiredbybeatz.com/en/radio-edit-for-club-tracks-how-to-get-your-track-on-the-radio/); a radio presenter: "around 3 minutes", go under rather than over, [Waterbear](https://waterbear.org.uk/blog/how-to-make-a-radio-edit-for-radio-play/)) describe radio programming and pop practice; trance's own radio edits run about 30 s longer.
- Caveats: MusicBrainz tags are applied by users (some "trance" tags will be trance-pop or Eurodance, e.g. Cascada), the catalogue leans to releases before 2020, and there is no Beatport or Discogs dataset (blocked from the sandbox).

**How the form is compressed** (sources plus arithmetic; no published bar-by-bar survey of trance radio edits was found):
- **DJ intro and outro go.** The 16-32 bars of drums at each end exist so DJs can mix ("eight bars of drums at the beginning so they can mix into it ... eight bars at the end so they can mix out of it", radio editor Bill Padley in [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/people/bill-padley-wise-buddamusic-ltd-music-production-recording-editing-radio-tv)); radio edits remove an intro build-up or halve a long intro ([Wikipedia: Radio edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_edit)), and end on a trimmed intro and a "hard ending" with nothing to fade from ([Mixgraph](https://www.mixgraph.io/glossary/radio-edit)). A radio presenter asks for an intro of "maybe 5 to 10 seconds ... don't make the intro 30 seconds long" ([Waterbear](https://waterbear.org.uk/blog/how-to-make-a-radio-edit-for-radio-play/)); a production guide wants the hook "in the first eight seconds" ([Inspired by Beatz](https://www.inspiredbybeatz.com/en/radio-edit-for-club-tracks-how-to-get-your-track-on-the-radio/), guide value).
- **The breakdown shrinks, it doesn't vanish.** "In a club it's great to have a big breakdown for two minutes ... on the radio, they don't do that", and the drums are often brought back early so the record doesn't break down to "a pad and a vocal" 20 seconds in (Padley, Sound On Sound). The paired arithmetic says the same: the DJ ends account for at most 32-64 of the ~108 bars cut, so the rest (about 45-75 bars) comes out of the breakdown, the builds and the drops. A breakdown of 16 bars (24 at most) fits; 32-64 doesn't. One of the club tracks in Attack's breakdown study (Hayden James, "Good Life") runs its breakdown as 24 bars, 1:39-2:29 ([Attack Magazine](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/the-breakdown/what-is-the-recipe-for-an-effective-breakdown/)).
- **Builds:** 8 bars is the radio size (club builds "can stretch over 32 bars", Inspired by Beatz). The snare roll fits it: quarters for two bars, 8ths for two, then 16ths and 32nds, doubling "every one or two bars"; the gap before the drop is one beat to two bars, and it need not be total silence (reverb tails, delays, a filtered sweep; [MusicRadar, 8 recipes for a drop](https://www.musicradar.com/how-to/8-classic-recipes-for-a-perfect-drop)).
- **Where the first drop lands:** no trance measurement was found. Derived: with the DJ intro gone, intro (8) + theme (16) + build (8) puts the first drop at bar 33, about **0:55** at 138 bpm; a short theme (8) brings it to about 0:42. For comparison, Billboard #1 hits reached their first chorus at 0:45 on average in 2024 ([Hit Songs Deconstructed](https://hitsongsdeconstructed.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/The-Hit-Songs-Deconstructed-1-Hit-Focus-Report-Highlights.pdf)). Aim for the first drop **between about 0:40 and 1:05** (bars 25-37).
- **How many drops and breakdowns fit:** two drops (a first drop and a main drop) around **one** breakdown. 120 bars hold intro 8, theme 16, build 8, drop 16, breakdown 16, build 8, main drop 32, outro 16. A second breakdown costs the first drop or the outro. Some guides cut the second drop altogether (Inspired by Beatz), but in trance the main drop after the breakdown is the release the track exists for (see "Ecstasy and euphoria"); keep it and shorten the first drop instead.
- **Rate of change** stays the genre's (something every 8-16 bars), but blocks of 8 replace blocks of 16 more often; a 32-bar section is the main drop's maximum.
- **Ending:** a hard end on the last downbeat, a short tail (a held chord, a reverb throw, 4-8 bars of thinning), or an early fade; never a 32-bar DJ outro.

**Spectre's radio shape** (a starting point; at 138 bpm, 120 bars = 3:29; allowed 3:00-4:00, aim 3:15-3:50, the measured middle half):

**Mastering: the same master as the extended mix.** Measured: of the pairs above, 70 have AcousticBrainz ReplayGain for both versions: the radio edit is a median **0.0 dB** louder than its long version (mean +0.03 dB, middle half -1.1 to +1.1 dB; both about -11 LUFS with the calibration in docs/CAROLING.md). That is consistent with the radio edit being cut from the same master (or one made the same way), with no loudness target of its own; the spread is most likely the arrangement (a radio edit has proportionally more drop and less intro). Radio editors work from the stereo master (Padley). Advice to deliver a quieter master (about -14 LUFS) to radio stations (Inspired by Beatz) concerns broadcast submissions with the station's own processor, not commercial releases. `trance_radio` therefore uses the `trance` master unchanged (-9 LUFS, -1 dBTP). Because a radio edit spends more of its time in drops, its integrated loudness lands closer to the drops' level and the limiter works harder; watch the PLR and the crest of the drops, not only the integrated number.

## Mixing and loudness
- **Kick and bass carve-up:** the offbeat placement is the first carve; the sidechain is the second. Keep everything below about 250 Hz narrow and the mids and highs wide.
- Supersaws high-passed (about 200 Hz) and wide; long, cavernous reverbs on leads and pads; snare and clap tails 0.5-1.5 s. Delays at 138 bpm: quarter 435 ms, dotted 8th 326 ms (`"beats": 0.75`), 8th 217 ms. Don't overuse delay on cycling arps.
- **Claps in trance** (researched 2026-11-24 after the owner heard session 007's clap as "someone knocking a piece of metal on wood", "way too loud"):
  - *What a clap is:* several hands slightly out of time. One hand clap lasts about 5 ms and is coloured by the air cavity between the hands, a resonance at about 0.5-1.6 kHz with a 90-375 Hz bandwidth depending on how the hands meet (Repp 1987; Peltola et al. 2007, Table 1). The TR-808 makes its clap from band-passed noise through two envelopes: a sawtooth that retriggers to give several quick bursts, and a longer one its service notes call the clap's "reverberation" (Roland TR-808 service notes); emulations put the bursts about 11 ms apart (KVR). Engine: `handclap` (several hands, each with its own resonance and noise, a short room). It never rings: a ringing (narrow, long-decaying) resonance or a comb filter is what reads as metal or wood.
  - *What recorded claps measure like* (measured 2026-11-24 after the owner heard the first `handclap` soloed as "a clock ticking"). Recordings (measured only, never stored in the repo or used in tracks): four claps from the `realclaps` folder of [tidalcycles/Dirt-Samples](https://github.com/tidalcycles/Dirt-Samples) (no licence stated for the folder) and the HandClap of AVL Drumkits 1.1 (CC-BY-SA; its five files are identical, so one clap) from [oramics/sampled](https://github.com/oramics/sampled); for reference, two Roland TR-808 claps, two TR-909 claps (oramics/sampled) and the two drum-machine-style claps in Dirt-Samples `cp`. No CC0 or public-domain clap was reachable: Wikimedia Commons, archive.org and Freesound are blocked from this sandbox, so two sources is a thin sample; more recordings (Robert can attach some) would firm up the numbers. Method: 1/3-octave spectrum of the first 300 ms relative to 1 kHz, 5 ms RMS envelope (300 Hz-10 kHz) relative to the peak, burst peaks, LPC and FFT resonances per burst (scripts not kept; the comparison figure is `examples/claps/handclap-vs-real.png`).
    - *Real claps:* several hand impacts in the first 20-40 ms, the loud ones within 0-25 ms and 6-12 ms apart, later ones quieter; each burst rings at 0.6-2.2 kHz with Q about 2-8 (LPC bandwidths 50-700 Hz: a little narrower than Peltola's single claps). The spectrum peaks at 1.25-1.6 kHz (+4 dB over 1 kHz), sits about -5 dB at 2.5-8 kHz and falls steeply above (-13 dB at 10 kHz, -18 at 12.5 kHz, -24 at 16 kHz); there is real body below 400 Hz (-9 to -15 dB at 100-250 Hz in the Dirt claps, -12 to -21 in the AVL clap). Centroid 1.7-2.2 kHz. The envelope never drops between the hands: -11 dB at 30 ms, -18 at 60 ms, -34 at 100 ms; 40 dB down after 70-115 ms, a tail time constant of 28-42 ms.
    - *Drum-machine claps* are a different animal: 3-4 retriggered bursts 11-13 ms apart, then a long noise "reverberation" (40 dB down after 120-300 ms), brighter (centroid 2.5-3.7 kHz, a flat top: -10 to -19 dB at 16 kHz) and with almost no low end (-31 to -51 dB at 100 Hz). Mean difference from the real claps: 7.0 dB (spectrum), 9.7 dB (envelope).
    - *The first `handclap` (model 1)* was a clock tick: 40 dB down after about 23 ms (the hands, then almost nothing), 5-7 dB too bright at 3-8 kHz (centroid 2.7 kHz), almost no body (-29 dB at 125 Hz). Mean difference from the real claps: 5.5 dB (spectrum), 5.9 dB (envelope).
    - *`handclap` model 2* (the default) was fitted to the mean of the real claps: the hands bunch in the first ~20 ms (each later hand quieter, time constant 22 ms), the Peltola resonances are scaled up 14% and a little narrower, a dense tail starts with the first hand (7 ms fade-in, 34 ms time constant, highs twice as fast), a 100-400 Hz flesh thump (39 ms) and a 11.4 kHz roll-off. Mean difference from the real claps: 1.0 dB (spectrum, 125 Hz-10 kHz) and 1.6 dB (envelope) with session 007's settings, 1-2 dB across 3-6 clappers at 6-12 ms; centroid 1.8 kHz, 40 dB down after about 100 ms. Left over: it is about 10 dB darker than the recordings at 16 kHz (the fit ignored the top octave, and darker is the safe side for the "static" complaint), and a single clapper (`clappers: 1`) matches the envelope less well (3.5 dB), as the recordings are several hands. A/B (synthesized only, soloed with First Light's clap settings, loudness-matched): `examples/claps/handclap-old.mp3` / `handclap-new.mp3`, dry: `handclap-old-dry.mp3` / `handclap-new-dry.mp3`.
  - *Level:* the kick is the loudest element and the clap or snare sits "a few dB below the kick drum level" (Loopmasters); a gain-staging guide starts kick and snare both peaking around -8 to -6 dBFS (Soundshock Audio). No measured survey of clap levels in trance was found, so this is engineer guidance. Spectre's practice since session 007 v029: **clap peaks about 6-10 dB under the kick's peaks** (`handclap` is spiky: its loudness sits about 18 dB under its peak (model 1: 19-22), so set it by the peak; check with the per-hit peaks, not with `stems_rms` alone). First Light v030 has them about 10 dB under. A clap peaking at the kick's level on 2 and 4 is the mix's crest spike and the first thing that reads as "too loud".
  - *Layering:* house and trance producers stack a snappy snare, a short mono clap and a wider clap, each nudged a few milliseconds off the grid (MusicRadar); "several short hits slightly out of time" is the classic synthesized clap, and doubled real claps thicken it (Sound On Sound). In the engine `handclap` already stacks the hands (`clappers`, `spread_ms`); for a layer, add a `snare` track about 6 dB below the clap or a second `handclap` with `hands: "flat"` panned and with a different `seed`.
  - *Reverb and EQ:* "a very gentle reverb ... for a sense of space" (Loopmasters); a bright ambience with about 60 ms pre-delay and a fast decay, or a short gated reverb; 1-2 kHz for snap (Sound On Sound); experimenting with reverb on the clap gives a track character (MusicRadar). In the engine: `room` in `handclap` for the short space, a reverb send of 0.2-0.3 for the 0.5-1.5 s trance tail, `hpf` 200-300 Hz. Don't saturate a clap to tame it: it turns the bursts into a click; lower it instead.
- **Loudness:** charting dance/electronic masters average -8.3 LUFS integrated; streaming normalizes to about -14 (see Configuration). In Spectre: `studio.py master vNNN --genre trance` (-9 LUFS). Louder masters hit the true-peak guard and stop gaining loudness.

## Vocals (researched 2026-11-24): weigh them per track
Using a voice is **the producer's choice for each track, never a requirement of the brief** (one exception so far, by the owner's request: session 013's brief makes a voice mandatory; see "Vocals in a radio edit"). Choose one of three ways and say why in the journal: instrumental, a sung vocal, or vocal fragments (chops). If you choose words, write your own lyrics to carry the feeling.

**How common each is (verified, not assumed).** Trance "is mostly instrumental, although vocals can be mixed in" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance_music)); "although much of trance music is purely instrumental, there are still quite a lot of trance tracks that use vocals" ([Native Instruments](https://blog.native-instruments.com/trance-music/)). How much depends on the scene. A count of credited singers in published year-end lists (Spectre's own count, 2026-11-24):
- *Mainstream (A State of Trance year-end countdowns):* at least 5 of the 2024 top 10 credit a singer (with Jaren, feat. Zoë Johnston, Ben Gold & Bo Bruce, feat. Ren Faye, ft. Lucy Pullin; [EDMTunes 2024](https://www.edmtunes.com/2024/12/the-top-50-tracks-on-a-state-of-trance-2024/)), and 4 of the 2025 top 5 (ft. Plumb, ft. SACHA, ft. Saskia Lie-Atjam, ft. Libby Whitehouse; [EDMTunes 2025](https://www.edmtunes.com/2025/12/watch-armin-van-asot-top-50/)). At the top of the mainstream, vocal tracks are the majority.
- *Underground uplifting and orchestral (the UpOnly "Tune of the Year" vote, 2013):* 2 of the 51 listed entries credit a vocalist ([Abora Recordings](https://www.abora-recordings.com/voting/toty/2013/2013_UpOnly_Vote_Results.pdf)). Here instrumentals are the norm, at about 96%.
- *Tech trance* has "minimal vocals" ([Insomniac](https://insomniac.com/magazine/10-trance-subgenres-you-need-to-know)). *Psytrance* samples films (science fiction, or horror in dark psy) more than it uses singers, and Goa grew from remixes that removed the lyrics ([Wikipedia: Psychedelic trance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_On)).
- So the owner's remark that many trance tracks have no vocals holds for underground uplifting, tech and psy, and not for the mainstream countdowns. Caveats: credits are a lower bound (uncredited vocals and chops aren't counted), the samples are small and come from different years and scenes, and no catalogue-wide count was reachable (Mixgraph gives vocal trance a 138 bpm median but no track counts; Beatport and Discogs block automated access).

**The three choices: when and why**
- *Instrumental:* the lead synth is the singer. The supersaw carries the hook and the theme, and in the breakdown a wordless line (`vox`, `vox2`) or a piano can state the theme slowly before the lead takes it at full power. Choose it when the feeling is wordless or bigger than words, for underground uplifting, tech or psy references, and when the track must work as a pure DJ tool. Keep the theme singable: a short phrase repeated with small changes, rising at the end of each 8 bars.
- *A sung vocal (vocal trance):* "grand, soaring, and operatic", "ethereal female leads floating amongst the synths", typically "mezzo-soprano to soprano female soloists" (male singers too), "mostly without a traditional verse/chorus structure"; pop-style vocals entered around the early 2000s ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance_music)). Trance "tends to favor the use of breathy, ambient, female vocals", and "more trance tracks now feature male vocalists as well" ([Native Instruments](https://blog.native-instruments.com/trance-music/)). Choose it when the feeling has something to say that the melody alone can't: a promise, a name, a direction (editorial, unsourced).
- *Fragments and chops:* snippets of a vocal sliced into "catchy hooks, lead lines, and even percussive elements", processed with reverb, delay, distortion, octave jumps and formant shifts ([Native Instruments: vocal chops](https://blog.native-instruments.com/vocal-chops/)); in trance, timestretched vocals as incidental effects, and a single vocal line chopped with its last words stretched "more and more" ([MusicRadar, 26 trance tips](https://www.musicradar.com/tuition/tech/26-trance-tips-34006), tips 15 and 24). Choose them when a human trace should colour the track without telling a story.

**Where and how much**
- The breakdown is the classic home: "maybe some kind of sultry vocal" over strings and effects (MusicRadar tip 16). In a vocal track the hook line usually returns in the drop with the lead doubling or answering it, and the DJ intro and outro stay free of vocals so DJs can blend. That arrangement habit is craft knowledge, unsourced.
- Sparse: phrases with air between them, words repeated rather than many lines. There is no verse/chorus requirement (Wikipedia). No sourced measure of how many bars carry a vocal was found.

**Processing (what producers do)**
- *Space:* vocals "have a lot of space to them": large reverbs (Native Instruments); long tails and pitched echoes build breakdown tension ([Attack Magazine](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/tutorials/creative-vocal-pitch-processing/)). Duck the reverb under the dry voice ("a sidechain compressor on your vocal's reverb") and EQ the delays so they don't swamp it ([MusicRadar, 96 vocal production tips](https://www.musicradar.com/tuition/tech/96-vocal-production-tips-437031), tips 66, 84, 92).
- *Up front:* "compress vocals quite heavily for dance records", "EQ them quite brutally"; vocals have to be prominent ([Sound On Sound, Secrets of house and trance](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/secrets-house-trance)); centre the lead vocal and duck competing parts rather than only turning the voice up (MusicRadar tips 37, 43, 68).
- *Doubling:* several takes make a natural chorus, and a doubled layer at the end of phrases lifts them; doublers and chorus effects thicken but cost punch (MusicRadar tips 7, 49, 91).
- *Pitch correction as a sound:* slow response sounds natural, fast "T-Pain-style" changes are the effect; heavy amounts don't sound natural (tips 52, 60).
- *Vocoder:* "vocoded vocals can be quite hard to make intelligible" (tip 30); keep it for texture, not for words.
- *Formant and pitch shifts* change the character of chops (an octave up, a formant shift; Native Instruments) and shape pitched echoes (Attack).
- *Breaths:* removing every breath "can make a vocal sound artificial" (MusicRadar tip 15).
- Light sidechain from the kick on a vocal in the drop is a common habit in club music, but no source was found for it (craft, unsourced).

**Lyrics.** No study of trance lyrics was found. Spectre's practice (editorial): few words, short phrases that fit the hook, repeated and varied rather than told as a story; concrete images (light, water, sky, home, holding on) that carry the feeling; vowels on the long notes ("light", "home", "free"), consonant clusters on short ones. Write lyrics that are yours and fit the brief's feeling, not a pastiche of famous lines.

**In the engine** (docs/MANUAL.md, "Voices"): `singer` with `"source": "tts"` for words (words sampled from a local TTS, the one neural part of Spectre, then autotuned onto the melody; for the processed, hard-tuned club vocal `"tune": 1, "retune_ms": 6, "vibrato": 0, "double": 0.7`; A/B in `examples/vocals/tts/`; the eSpeak sources `breathy` and `pulse` sound robotic), `vox2` for wordless lines and chops (`onset` consonants give "da", "na", "la" fragments), `vox` for the old wordless "ooh". Settings that suit trance: `send_reverb` 0.3-0.4 and `send_delay` 0.2 (dotted 8th) on the voice, a light kick sidechain (depth 0.2-0.3, release 150 ms) in the drop, `hpf` 150-180 Hz, and lyrics between A4 and D5 (the low half of the mezzo-soprano range; an octave below where the supersaw plays the same hook). Above E5 words turn into chirps (a limit of the voice itself as well as of the engine; see MANUAL "Register"), and syllables want a quarter note or more at 138 bpm. A/B renders: `examples/vocals/`; the diagnostic ladder: `examples/vocals/diagnostic/`. **Since 2026-11-24 sung words use `singer` `"source": "svs"`** (DiffSinger voices, approved by the owner by ear; docs/MANUAL.md "Voices"), with `tts` kept for male voices; see "Vocals in a radio edit" below for the svs voices that suit trance.

### Vocals in a radio edit (researched 2026-11-24, for session 013)
A brief may make a voice mandatory (session 013 does); then the choice above is between chops, a short hook line and a pad or choir texture, never instrumental, and never a sung song (standing rule 13).

**How much vocal.** No measured count of vocal bars in trance radio edits exists. What can be measured: a featured singer is credited on 15% of the 1,072 trance radio edits on MusicBrainz, against 14% of extended mixes and 11% of original mixes (Spectre's count, 2026-11-24; credits are a lower bound, chops and uncredited voices aren't counted). So radio edits are not a vocal-only format: an instrumental or chop-based trance single gets a radio edit too. What changes is density: with the DJ ends gone, whatever voice the track has is a larger share of its 3:30. Radio editors also push in the other direction: a dance record has "not enough in it, most of the time, to sustain more than three minutes", and a vocal effect that "went on for about 30 seconds" was cut to five (Padley, [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/people/bill-padley-wise-buddamusic-ltd-music-production-recording-editing-radio-tv)). Spectre's rule (editorial): the voice appears in every section except the build gaps, but as one short idea at a time, and the hook fragment is heard by the first drop.

**Where it sits when the form is compressed**
- *Early:* the track must say what it is in the first 5-15 s (Waterbear; Inspired by Beatz's "first eight seconds"), so a radio edit can open on the voice: a processed chop or a fragment of the hook line, filtered or far away, before the kick. In the extended form the DJ intro must stay free of vocals; in the radio edit there is no DJ intro to protect.
- *Not alone too early:* don't strip to "a pad and a vocal" in the first 20 s (Padley); keep a pulse under an early voice.
- *Builds:* the voice is a tension layer, not the lead: a frozen reverb tail of the last word (decay about 4 s, pre-delay low), a high-pass that starts thin and opens toward the drop, a gate or tremolo whose pulses slow or speed toward the drop, trimmed to end one or two bars before it, "sitting inside the mix as a tension layer, not dominating it" ([Soundbridge](https://www.soundbridge.io/vocal-transition-trick-for-breakdowns-and-drops)); the gap before the drop can be filled with a reverb or delay tail instead of silence (MusicRadar, 8 recipes).
- *Drops:* chops as rhythmic hooks, lead lines and percussion ([Native Instruments: vocal chops](https://blog.native-instruments.com/vocal-chops/)); a hook line answered or doubled by the lead (craft, as above). In a 32-bar main drop the voice works as a call (2-4 bars) answered by the supersaw, not over all 32 bars.
- *Breakdown:* the classic place for the line or the exposed voice; in Attack's breakdown example "vocal chops are used before the breakdown but the ones in the breakdown are much more ambient", and the vocals return with the kick on a riser ([Attack Magazine](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/the-breakdown/what-is-the-recipe-for-an-effective-breakdown/)). In a 16-bar radio breakdown there is room for one phrase stated and one answered.

**Processing: drops against breakdowns**
- *Drops:* dry-ish, close, hard: "compress vocals quite heavily for dance records", "EQ them quite brutally" ([Sound On Sound, Secrets of house and trance](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/secrets-house-trance)); short chops gated to the 16th grid, pitched (an octave up, a formant shift) or distorted (Native Instruments); reverb ducked under the dry voice (MusicRadar tip 66); a light kick sidechain (craft, unsourced). The voice must cut through supersaws: brighter voices and presence, not more level.
- *Breakdowns:* wet and far: long reverbs, pitched echoes and long tails build tension ([Attack Magazine, creative vocal pitch processing](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/tutorials/creative-vocal-pitch-processing/)), ambient chops (Attack), stacked doubles and choir layers for the pad texture.
- *Builds:* the transition chain above (frozen tail, opening high-pass, accelerating or slowing gate, the reverb bloom at the last moment before the drop).

**The svs voices for tense, dark or ethereal trance** (from docs/voice/VOICES.md and engine/voices.json; auditions in `examples/vocals/svs/voices/`; write A3-D5, sweet spot F#4-B4; words want a quarter note or more at 138 bpm, so chops are short syllables or a held vowel cut by a `gate`):
| Use | Voice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Ethereal pad, breakdown air, the far voice | `hanami-soft` (soft, dark, airy; tilt -19 dB), `leona-soft` (breathy, breath 0.18), `leona-soft` with `gender` -0.5 (the darkest, breathiest texture measured) | they don't cut through saws, so breakdowns, intros and under-layers only |
| Dark choir, low dread, solemn texture | `hanami-dark` (formants lowered; tilt -28 dB) | words blur (Whisper misheard it), so use it for vowels and choir layers, not for the hook's words |
| The hook line with words | `hanami` (clear, natural) or `hanami-power` (bright, firm; tagged for trance hooks and calls over dense mixes) | intelligible; `hanami` is warm (tender by default): tension has to come from harmony, rhythm and processing |
| Chops in the drops, stabs, calls | `hanami-power`, `leona-power` (brightest, hard, edgy; tagged for EDM/trance drops) | they cut through supersaws; `leona-power` slurs words (fine for a vowel or one word) |

- **Choirs:** several voices a third or sixth apart, other modes and `seed`s (`examples/vocals/svs/choir-line-dry.mp3`); all are female, so a low choir layer goes on `choir`/`vox2` or instruments. `vox2` (pure synthesis) can add wordless chops beside the svs voice.

Every svs voice used goes into the README's **Credits** line and the site notes with its `credit` from engine/voices.json, e.g. "Vocals: Hoshino Hanami (DiffSinger voicebank by Lotte V, lottev.moe), Potluck Vocoder by tigermeat" (LEONA: "Vocals: LEONA (DiffSinger voicebank by Lotte V, lottev.moe, CC BY 4.0), Potluck Vocoder by tigermeat").

## Mixing the voice (researched 2026-11-24)
"Vocals" above says where a voice goes and what it is for; this section is how to mix it so the words survive supersaws, a kick and a loud master. The principles for every genre, the choir research and the check procedure are in [docs/voice/MIXING.md](voice/MIXING.md). Values marked *(hand-tuned)* are Spectre's starting points, not sourced numbers. Published interviews with dance engineers describe the approach but almost never give settings, and no measured survey of trance vocal levels or reverb times was found.

**What the research says**
- **Loud, bright, squeezed, mixed last.** Darren Tate: "I'll tend to compress vocals quite heavily for dance records", "EQ them quite brutally", and he needs "the vocals sitting up there in the mix"; he balances the track first and fits the vocal last ([Sound On Sound, Secrets of house and trance](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/secrets-house-trance)). Armin van Buuren makes the room in the instruments instead: "Only use the sounds in the frequency ranges you want them to be active in", because "I want to hear what the vocalist is singing" ([Sound On Sound, 2009](https://www.soundonsound.com/people/armin-van-buuren)). One documented in-the-box dance-pop lead ran a compressor at 6:1 with light de-essing built in, saturation on the voice throughout, and reverb and delay sends only from the lead; the chorus chops got their own group with a rhythmic volume shaper at 37% for "a little bounce" ([Sound On Sound, Inside Track: Clean Bandit](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/inside-track-clean-bandit-solo?page=3)).
- **Space is long, but it is kept off the words.** Large reverbs and long tails are the genre (sources under "Processing" above). What keeps the words clear under them is general practice: a pre-delay from 30-40 ms to over 100 ms keeps the dry voice in front, the reverb is high-passed around 150 Hz and often low-passed at 3-4 kHz, and its level is found by pulling it down until the voice separates from the track and then coming halfway back ([Sound On Sound, Vocal Production](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/vocal-production)); the reverb and delay sends are de-essed so sibilants don't splash ([Sound On Sound, Managing Sibilance](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/managing-sibilance)); the reverb is ducked under the dry voice (MusicRadar tip 66, above). Excess reverb and delay as spot effects on single words ("throws") are a dance habit (Vocal Production); the Despacito remix used quarter- and 8th-note throws on single words only ([Tingen](https://tingen.org/making-of-bieber-despacito-remix/)).
- **Level:** measured lead-vocal levels exist for chart pop (about 1-3 dB over the accompaniment), rock (0 dB) and metal (-3 dB), not for dance music ([Gerdes & Siedenburg 2023](https://www.geidai.ac.jp/~marui/course_enshu_2024/gerdes_siedenburg_202304.pdf)). A trance drop is as dense as a rock chorus, a breakdown as open as a ballad.
- **Chops are rhythm, not text** (Native Instruments, above). Nobody mixes a chop to be read; a chop that must carry a word has to be mixed as a line.
- **Mastering.** At -9 to -10 LUFS the limiter and glue compressor work on every kick. That shortens the distance between the dry voice and everything quieter than it: reverb tails, delays and the saws between the syllables all come up. A reverb level that just works before the master is too much after it, so judge words on the master, not on the premaster. High-shelf moves on the master raise sibilance with them (Managing Sibilance); the master's tonal EQ moves the voice's presence with the saws', it cannot separate them.

**The engine, measured:** `mix.reverb` `size` 0.75 decays in 2.1 s, 0.85 in 3.0 s, 0.88 in about 3.5 s, 0.9 in 4.0 s (T30 through the bus filters). There is one reverb bus and one delay bus, no ducking of the returns from the voice, no de-esser, and `sidechain` only listens to drum tracks. So "keep the reverb off the words" is done with pre-delay, small sends on word tracks, and separate throw tracks.

**Starting chains** (all *hand-tuned* except the blend-d values from MANUAL "Voices"):

- **A line with words in a drop (`line`):** nearly dry, long pre-delay, a bright voice (`hanami-power`; `leona-power` slurs words), 4:1 or more. The long tail comes from the `throw` track on the last word, so the tail starts when the words are over. Level about 0 to -1 LU against the band in the drop *(hand-tuned, between the measured rock and pop figures)*; make room in the band rather than adding level: `{"type": "eq", "hz": 2500, "gain_db": -2.5, "q": 0.7}` on the supersaws, a section `auto` of -2 dB on saws and lead for the bars of the call, or a call-and-answer arrangement where the saws rest while the voice sings. Kick sidechain on a word line light (0.1-0.2) or none: deeper pumping swallows syllables that fall on the beat.
- **The far voice in a breakdown or intro (`far`):** wet is the point, but keep the pre-delay at 80-120 ms and the send near 0.3 with a 3 s reverb; 2-4 LU over the pads under it. **Never sweep a low-pass or high-pass across words** (a filter under about 6 kHz removes the s, t, f and k that make them words; session 012's door did exactly this): filter the reverb's throw, the pad or a wordless double, and bring the worded voice in by level.
- **Chops (`chop`):** a syllable of two 16ths at 138-140 bpm lasts 0.21 s, under the quarter note a sung word needs (MANUAL "Voices"). Mix them as rhythm: short fades, heavy compression, gated to the grid, delay for bounce. If the chop repeats the hook's words, let the full line state them once, clearly, before the chops take over, so the ear knows what the fragments are.
- **Choir and pad voices:** MIXING.md "Choir and ensemble voices": `svs_vibrato` 0.3-0.5, vowels only, `hanami-dark` and `leona-soft` welcome here, sends 0.5-0.7, deep kick sidechain (0.6-0.8) in drops, 6-10 LU under the lead element, high-passed at 220-250 Hz and dipped at 2.5-3 kHz so they stay out of the word line's band.
- **Delay:** dotted 8th (`beats` 0.75) or quarter (`beats` 1); a word line sends 0.03-0.06, because each repeat lands on the next word; the throw sends 0.5-0.7.
- **De-essing substitute:** the high shelf at 8 kHz stays at 3 dB or less on word lines, the reverb bus `lpf` at 7-8 kHz and the delay bus at 4 kHz; the throw track is low-passed at 5 kHz before its sends.

**How to check:** Whisper (small.en) per sung line on the **mastered** mix and on the voices alone (session 013's `words.py` does the first; procedure in MIXING.md "Checking a voice"), and the vocal-versus-band loudness per section from a render with the voice tracks muted. Check lines only: chops are not expected to transcribe.

**Session 013, "Half of Me" (the owner: "places where he can't understand the words").** The producer measured v026: Whisper heard 4 of the 36 sung words (11%) on the master while the same lines dry were understood, so the words are lost in the space and the band, not in the voice. What the project shows against the research above: the reverb is 3.5 s (`size` 0.88) and the worded tracks send 0.2 (`line`) and 0.42 (`far`) into it; the delay bus feeds back 0.42; the intro's `far` voice enters through a 4 kHz low-pass that opens to 9 kHz across the words; the chops sing four words on two 16ths each with `leona-power`; and the master runs at -9.3 LUFS. The long reverb is right for the genre (the research does not support "less reverb" here); what is missing is the separation of words from tail: the `line` and `throw` chains above, no filter sweep on worded voices, room in the saws under the two calls, and the check run on the master.

## Ecstasy and euphoria: how trance gets there
- **The break routine** (breakdown, build-up, drop) is the engine of euphoria. In a 2019 study of EDM peak experiences, listeners described intense, energising, uplifting and embodied responses, attributed mostly to the drop. Drop sections produced significantly higher skin conductance than the rest of the break routine.
- **Expectation:** pleasure comes from expectations being fulfilled, delayed or violated, which can produce chills and an intense desire to move. The drop should come exactly when the body expects it, but with *more* than expected: the lead an octave up, a sus chord resolving, a new top note, a key change, the kick returning after a beat of silence.
- **The collective moment:** goosebumps and the urge to raise arms come from "planet-sized major chord riffs" and emotive melodies. The breakdown's chord change, where the progression turns from minor weight to a major lift, is the "hands in the air" moment that a crowd shares. Ecstasy here is self-dissolving and communal: being lifted out of yourself together.
- **For an ecstasy brief:** build the release through the arc, not just the drop. The breakdown should feel like holding your breath with everyone; the drop is the exhale. Keep the body (kick, pump, rolling bass) and the sky (supersaw, pads, reverb) both present at the peak.

### Nearest wrong feelings
- **Cheesy:** a stock progression, stock arp, stock riser, everything predictable including the sounds, or "happy" with no shadow. Fix: one unexpected chord colour (sus, add9, a borrowed chord), restraint in the breakdown, a melody with a real shape.
- **Hollow festival EDM:** a big-room drop that is just a kick, a stab and a shout, with no melody or harmony carrying the release; builds that promise and deliver nothing. Ecstasy in trance is melodic and harmonic.
- **Aggressive hard dance:** above 145 bpm, distorted kicks, hammering minor stabs, a rave alarm. That is an attack; ecstasy is open.
- **Chill or ambient:** a breakdown that never returns, no kick release, floating without a body. The rush needs the body back.
- **Melancholy "sad trance":** minor progressions that reach up and never land read as longing (the same trap as session 001). The lift has to arrive.

## Mastering (industry practice)
Researched 2026-11-24 under standing rule 8. The proposed configuration, the engine tests and every source are in [docs/proposals/mastering-trance-poprock.md](proposals/mastering-trance-poprock.md). **Measured** means a published measurement; **opinion** means engineer or platform guidance. No trance-only loudness or spectrum survey was found: the measured numbers are for dance/electronic charts and hits in general.

**Measured**
- **Loudness:** the 10 top Hot Dance/Electronic songs (2025) averaged -8.3 LUFS integrated (range -11.3 to -6.2), with short-term max averaging -6.1 (up to -4.5) and LRA 3.5-13.8 LU (average 6.6). True peaks reached up to +1.5 dBTP; "Beautiful People" has a PLR of 7.5 dB ([iZotope 2025](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/dance-electronic-analysis)). Global top-10 hits (H1 2024) averaged -8.3 ±1 LUFS, short-term max -6 ±1, with 80% peaking at or above 0 dBTP ([iZotope 2024](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/mastering-trends)). In 100 Beatport tracks from top labels, true peaks ran from +0.01 to +5.5 dBTP ([Dance Music NW 2018](https://www.dancemusicnw.com/electronic-music-mastering-lufs-and-true-peak-explained/), the author's own measurement). Hits since 2010 average a PLR of 9.3 dB ([Țînț & Drăgulin 2025](https://webbut.unitbv.ro/index.php/Series_VIII/article/view/11335)).
- **Normalization:** Spotify turns everything down to -14 LUFS. It raises quieter tracks only as far as 1 dB of true-peak headroom allows. In "Loud" mode (-11) it applies its own limiter ([Spotify](https://support.spotify.com/artists/article/loudness-normalization/)). An iZotope author estimates that about 85% of casual listeners have normalization on. So mastering at -9 instead of -8.3 costs **nothing** on normalized streaming: both are turned down. It matters only where nothing normalizes (DJ decks, SoundCloud, downloads), and there it is a 0.7 dB difference.
- **Tonal balance:** "electronic" has an "unmistakeable" extended low end and is among the brightest genres (Pestana et al. 2013). Spectra over the decades got flatter, with more extended lows and a lower bass resonance. Mixgraph rates trance "brightness" as high (34 tracks, unitless). More percussion means relatively more bass and treble (Elowsson & Friberg 2017). No numeric modern trance spectrum is published (iZotope's targets exist only as plugin presets).
- **Width:** 2024 hits averaged -8.5 LU side minus mid (middle half -10.9 to -6). Pop is widest. There is no trance figure.

**Opinion (engineers, platforms)**
- Club masters run about -7 to -6 LUFS short-term at the drop (Dance Music NW, Toolroom and Dirtybird references). For melodic techno, about -7 LUFS integrated. Spread clipping over stages of at most 1-2 dB each, with transparent limiting in breakdowns and harder limiting in drops. Keep kick, sub and bass in phase and mono-compatible, focus the low end at 50-120 Hz, and check in mono because club subs are mono ([Nihil Young, NI](https://blog.native-instruments.com/mastering-with-nihil-young/)).
- True peak: keep under -1 dBTP, or -2 dBTP for masters louder than -14 LUFS (Spotify). Robjohns sees an argument for -3 dBTP with lossy codecs ([Sound On Sound 2019](https://www.soundonsound.com/sound-advice/q-how-loud-should-master-tracks-intended-streaming)). Loud masters show true peaks of +1 to +2 dBTP and clip in encoders ([Ian Shepherd](https://productionadvice.co.uk/spotify-upload-true-peak/)).
- Genre habits: the kick-and-offbeat-bass carve and the sidechain pump leave low-end room and are preserved by mastering, not added there. Buzzy supersaws mask some distortion, so trance tolerates limiting better than sparse music (iZotope 2025, on "Forever Young"). Center the bass and widen the top ([Sound On Sound 2001](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/increasing-loudness-dance-music-production)).

**For Spectre (proposal; genres.json not yet changed)**
- `--lufs -9` (lands about -9.3). Use -8.5 only when the mix controls the kick's peaks. Keep `--tp -1`, mono below 120 Hz, and aim width at -9 to -6 LU. Tested on `examples/trance-test.json`: the engine's true-peak guard is not the limit, the soft clipper is. Today's -10 already shaves 6.8 dB off clubkick peaks, -9 shaves 8.0 dB, and -8.5 shaves 8.7 dB (PLR 7.7). A 320 kbps MP3 stays under -0.5 dBTP. **Producers:** clip or saturate the kick or drum bus in the mix so the master doesn't have to.
- Proposed curve `trance_modern` = the measured `trance` with era offsets: +2 dB at 31.5-50 Hz, +1.5 at 63 Hz, +1 at 10 kHz, +2 at 12.5 kHz, +3.5 at 16 kHz. These are estimates (±2-4 dB), pending measurement of owned reference tracks.

## What session 007 learned
- Hold the hook back (80 bars in an extended mix) and keep the first drop in a smaller room; the release can then be one changed note.
- True silence (`exit silence`) for the bar before a release; a breakdown that starts from almost nothing.
- Clubkick around 56 Hz, narrow deep cut at 66 Hz, boost at 105 Hz; saturate the kick last. Watch the clap: layered with the kick on 2 and 4 it can be the crest spike.
- The owner's verdict on the released v028 was "the song is really nice and promising but those claps destroy it". The ears missed it: the old `clap` with `"spread": 0.6` (seconds, not ms) was one 4 ms click plus a second click as loud as the hit 0.4-1.7 beats later, off the beat, turned up 10 dB and saturated. v029 replaced it with `handclap`, peaking 6.5-9 dB under the kick. Check a clap's solo and per-hit peaks, not only the overview.
- Darken open hats (shelf from 9 kHz, low-pass at 12k) so groove-only stretches don't read as hiss on phones.
