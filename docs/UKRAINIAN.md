# Ukrainian folk for trap: a working brief

For sessions that build on Ukrainian traditional music. A vocabulary, not a rulebook.

## The modern reference
Ukraine's electro-folk and ethno-hip-hop scene: Go_A (folktronica, "white voice" vocals with sopilka), ONUKA (electronic drums with bandura, sopilka, trombone and horns), Kalush (folk-rap: ancient folk with hip-hop), DakhaBrakha ("ethno-chaos"). Folk instruments carry the weight in these mixes; they are never decoration.

## Instruments (and their engine names)
- **Trembita**: Hutsul long wooden horn from the Carpathians, 2.5-8 m long; pitch comes from breath and lips (natural harmonics). Historically a signal instrument (calls across mountains, news of a death, gatherings) → `trembita`
- **Lira** (hurdy-gurdy): wheel-bowed drone instrument of the wandering lirnyky, who sang dumy → `lira` (long notes as drones, `buzz` for the trompette)
- **Bandura / kobza**: plucked; the kobzari sang the dumy (epic laments) to them → `cimbalom` with damp 0.5-0.8, `pluck`, or `oud` bright
- **Tsymbaly** (hammered dulcimer) → `cimbalom`
- **Sopilka** (duct flute), **floyara** → `nai` (bright, chiffy) or `ney`
- **Skrypka** (violin), **basolia** (3-string bass) → `fiddle`, `bass`
- **Bubon / taraban** (frame drum), **resheto** (tambourine) → `tom`, `darbuka` doum, shakers, claps
- **White voice (bilyi holos)**: open, chest-register, bright and loud folk singing, often in 3-4 part village harmony → `choir` (vowel "ah" or "ee") with stacked open fifths and fourths. *Correction (2026-11-25): stacked open fifths and fourths are not the documented Ukrainian texture; village part-singing is unison, heterophony (seconds and thirds that close to a unison), a drone in the north, or a group "bass" with a solo upper voice that cadences at the octave. See "Granularity", Polyphony.*

## Modes and harmony
- **Ukrainian Dorian / Dorian #4 ("duma mode")**: 1 2 b3 #4 5 6 b7, e.g. D E F G# A B C. The mode of the dumy, the epic laments sung with bandura, kobza or lira. Often modulates with Phrygian dominant.
- **Minor modes with augmented seconds** are the most striking feature of authentic Ukrainian folk music. *Qualified (2026-11-25): Grove (Hrytsa) finds most ritual songs narrow-range and diatonic and traditional music "greatly influenced by the Western major-minor system"; the augmented second belongs to the Hutsul mode (Grove) and the duma mode (above), not to every region or genre. See "Granularity".* Mixolydian and Dorian are used more than plain major and minor; anhemitonic pentatonic appears in spring songs (vesnianky).
- Village polyphony: drones, parallel open fifths, heterophony, a high voice cutting above the rest. *("Parallel open fifths" is not supported by the polyphony sources read on 2026-11-25; the rest is, region by region: see "Granularity".)*

## Rhythms
- **Kolomyika** (Hutsul/Carpathian): fast 2/4 with a short driving melody phrase, 4+4+6 syllable lines. *(Also slow: Hutsul protyazhni songs use the same 4+4+6 couplet; Grove.)*
- **Hopak**: energetic, leaping 2/4. Beware: at high tempo it reads as celebration or as martial pride.
- **Dumy**: free, recitative, rubato over a drone. The emotional core of Ukrainian lament.
- Laments (holosinnia) at wakes: solo, keening, falling lines.

## For the anti-war statement (session 005)
The statement: war is wrong, said plainly and loudly. The risk: Ukrainian martial and Cossack imagery (marches, hopak leaps, battle horns, triumphant brass) can glorify war, which is the opposite of the brief. The statement lives in the arc and in the sound: grief and fury at the cost, folk life and voices that refuse to be erased, war sounds that get confronted and drowned out.
Nearest wrong feelings: a battle anthem or martial pride, triumph, pure sadness or a passive lament, rage at a specific enemy (the statement is against war itself), war-film spectacle.

## Granularity: regions, layers, eras and the current practice (researched 2026-11-25; docs/PRODUCTION.md section 5, item 10)
The folk side of this brief, split along the dimensions in which Ukrainian traditional music really varies. Source keys in [brackets] are listed at the end of this section; [H] marks a hand-tuned value with no source. The trap side (beat, 808, master target of a trap record) is a separate brief.

### The dimensions, and why the boundaries are where they are
1. **Region (song-dialect zone).** Ukrainian ethnomusicology maps polyphony types onto ethnographic zones, from expedition recordings of 1980-2010 [Skaz]: *monophony* in the south-west (the Carpathians and adjacent lands, western and Transnistrian Podillia), *drone diaphony and archaic heterophony* in the north (Polissia, the Ukrainian-Belarusian border), *two-register heterophony* and *functional two-part singing* in the Middle Dnipro region and the left bank, *developed heterophony* in the Polissia-Dnipro transition zone, Central and Eastern Ukraine (the steppe included). The boundaries follow geography and settlement history, not oblast borders: the forest-and-marsh north and the mountains were remote and kept archaic singing; the forest-steppe Podillia sits between them and has transitional forms; the Middle Dnipro and the left bank border the steppe, were settled later and reshaped the style [Skaz, Conclusions]. Grove names the same split: north/north-west (Polissia, Volyn), the left bank, the Carpathians (Boykos, Hutsuls, Lemkos as three distinct musics) [Grove §2-3]. Slobozhanshchyna and Bukovina are listed below as sub-zones with thin data.
2. **Genre layer (inside every region).** The same singers keep an *early* layer (calendar and wedding songs: narrow range, unison, heterophony, drone) and a *later* layer (lyrical songs: wider range, two-part bass plus solo upper voice) side by side: "song genres of different times of formation have different types of polyphony" [Skaz]. A brief that names a region should also name the layer (a Polissian spring song and a Polissian lyric song are different sounds).
3. **Kobzar and lirnyk tradition.** Not a region but a professional stratum (blind itinerant singers in guilds, mainly central and eastern Ukraine [Grove §4]): its own forms (dumy, psalms, historical songs) and instruments.
4. **Timeline and scene.** Village tradition; the Soviet stage ensembles (from the late 1930s); the authentic-singing revival (from the 1980s); ethno-fusion (2004-2019); the wartime scene (2022-2026). Boundaries are where the sound and the institutions changed (below).

### The shared core (holds in every region and era)
- **Group singing a cappella, by gender:** most genres are ensemble songs without instruments; women sing the spring-summer and wedding cycles, young men the winter rounds (carols, Malanka) and may add a violin doubling the tune [Skaz §2]. **shared**
- **The cadence closes to one pitch:** whatever the texture (heterophony, drone, bass plus upper voice), the stanza ends in unison or at the octave [Skaz]. **shared** (this is the most portable rule for a producer: open intervals during the line, a unison at its end).
- **Chest register, unvibrated, open throat** (the "white" sound) for the group; head voice only for special solo parts [Grove §2(ii); Skaz; CREES]. **shared; varies** in how high and how loud.
- **Strophic forms with refrains and line repetition:** two- or three-line stanzas (AB, AAB, ABB) and a reprise in lyric songs; calendar songs of one or two lines with refrains; the line of the previous stanza repeated to start the next one in left-bank long songs [Grove §1, §2]. **shared**
- **Text drives rhythm:** syllable counts per line (5+5 carols, 4+4+6 kolomyika, 5+3 to 7+3 wedding ladkannia), accents of the text set against the metre in dance songs [Grove]. **shared**
- **Dance music accelerates during the piece** [Grove §1]. **shared** (instrumental and dance-song genres)
- **Endings held, last unstressed syllable cut:** long pause on the last stressed syllable, the last unstressed one shortened (documented for Polissia [Grove §2(ii)]; heard widely, but only sourced there). **varies**

Each row: the element, the value, **shared** or **varies** (how much), source. Tempi are not given by any source read; where a row says "slow" or "fast" that is the source's word.

**Polissia (north: Volyn, Rivne, Zhytomyr, northern Kyiv and Chernihiv regions; the Desna and Pripyat basins)**

**Carpathians: Hutsuls, Boykos, Lemkos (with Bukovina and Transcarpathia)**
| element | value | shared/varies | source |
|---|---|---|---|
| texture | **monophony**: solo songs in parlando rubato; group songs in unison | varies (the south-west only) | [Grove §3], [Skaz] |
| melody | microtonal descending lines with a glissando at the stanza end, like shepherds' tunes on sopilka or drymba | varies | [Grove §3] |
| calls | **hoekannia**: solo shepherds' message songs, yodel-like, in the foothills | varies | [Grove §3] |
| lament | **holosinnia**, recitative-like, once found throughout Ukraine, kept here | shared in origin, now varies | [Grove §3] |

| ensemble | **troisti muzyky** ("three musicians"): violin + sopilka + buben, violin + tsymbaly + buben, or violin + accordion + buben, improvising on dance tunes; solo singing with the band at weddings | shared nationally, strongest here | [Grove §1, §3] |

**Kobzar and lirnyk tradition (central and eastern Ukraine; a guild stratum, not a region)**
| element | value | shared/varies | source |
|---|---|---|---|
| form | **dumy**: declamatory, non-strophic; lines of 6 to 18 syllables in irregular groups (*ustupy*); can exceed 300 lines | varies | [Grove §4] |
| opening | an exclamation "oy" or "hey" on a descending phrase, the **zaplachka** ("weeping"), which holds the motif the kobza or lira then varies through the piece | varies | [Grove §4] |
| cadences | an ornamented cadence at the end of each ustup | varies | [Grove §4] |
| instruments | kobza, bandura, lira (hurdy-gurdy); the singer accompanies himself | varies | [Grove §4], [Kobzar] |
| other repertoire | begging songs, psalms, historical songs, satirical songs | varies | [Kobzar] |
| institution | guilds with 3-6 years of apprenticeship and an exam; ended in the mid-1930s under Stalin (reports of a congress in Kharkiv whose attendees were executed are not documented in the Soviet press); Soviet bandurists worked in ensembles from approved texts; the Kobzar Guild revival recovers oral practice; "kobza and wheel lyre tradition" on UNESCO's Register of Good Safeguarding Practices, 2024 | | [Kobzar] |

**Mode note across regions:** the "Ukrainian Dorian" (1 2 b3 #4 5 6 b7) above is the duma mode; the Hutsul mode has a raised 7th as well [Grove §3]; calendar songs are narrow, diatonic, "major or minor" [Grove §2(i)]; chant and dance melodies "come close to Western diatonic and functional-harmonic models" [Grove §1]. So: augmented second = Carpathian or duma colour; narrow diatonic = ritual; major-minor with a leading tone = later lyric, romance and march songs.

### Current practice (2023-2026): a checklist for briefs that ask for the newest sound
| element | where it comes from | engine today |
|---|---|---|
| a regional source song named and kept recognisable (a vesnianka, a kolomyika, a Polissian wedding song), not a generic "Slavic" mood | Go_A "Shum", DakhaBrakha | yes: notes and modes are free |
| the white voice in front: chest register, no vibrato, loud, sharp; with the head-voice "thin" solo an octave up as the second colour | Go_A; [Skaz] | **partly**: no Ukrainian-language singer; `vox2` with `rd` near 0.3 (pressed), vibrato 0 and `ee`/`ah` vowels can carry wordless calls; `choir` for the group |
| the "gu" glissando call at the end of a spring-song stanza; hoekannia-style calls | [Grove §2(i), §3] | yes: `vox2` with `~` glides |
| a pipe hook: sopilka (and the hole-less overtone flute telenka) | Go_A, Kalush | **approximate**: `nai` or `ney`; no duct-flute overblowing model, no telenka |
| bandura and tsymbaly as rhythm, not ornament | ONUKA | **approximate**: `cimbalom` (tsymbaly: good), bandura via `cimbalom`/`pluck`/`oud` |
| trembita calls on natural harmonics between sections | ONUKA | yes: `trembita` |
| a dance-music accelerando (the troisti muzyky habit) built into the form | [Grove §1]; Go_A | yes: section `bpm` ramps (docs/MANUAL.md "Tempo changes") |
| heterophony, not chords: two or three sung lines that split by seconds and thirds and close to a unison or octave | [Skaz] | **by hand**: write separate `vox2`/`choir` tracks with their own lines; no heterophony generator |
| an electronic frame chosen per act: dark techno (Go_A), folk-rap over hip-hop drums (Kalush), acoustic ethno-chaos with cello, djembe and accordion (DakhaBrakha) | as named | techno and trap drums, cello (`violin`/`strings` family): yes; **accordion: missing**; djembe: approximate (`tom`, `darbuka`) |
| village and archive voices as material | [Europavox], [ACL] | **no, by design**: the engine synthesises every sound (docs/agents/project.md); evoke, don't sample |

**Missing for this tradition (to build or to find):** bandura (many treble strings plucked with the nails), sopilka with overblown register and telenka, drymba (jew's harp; also wanted for Latvia), buben (frame drum with jingles), accordion or bayan, duda/koza (bagpipe: `dudas` approximates), a properly bowed folk fiddle in Hutsul style (`violin` exists, its idiom is the lăutar one). **Voices:** no DiffSinger bank sings Ukrainian; no hard, shouting or "white" voice of either sex; no male bass for Cossack songs ([docs/voice/VOICES.md](voice/VOICES.md) "What is missing"). **Licence flag:** the Lotte V voices (`hanami*`, `leona*`) forbid "political" use; an anti-war statement like session 005 should not use them for words without the owner's decision (VOICES.md, licence table).
