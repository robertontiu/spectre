## The voice that sings it
- **`singer` `"source": "svs"` with `hanami-es` or `hanami-es-soft`** (Hoshino Hanami by Lotte V, Root and Nectar modes; `"lang": "es"` set by the voice id). Hanami was trained on about 7 minutes of Spanish among 3 h 53 min of data in eight languages, all sung by the same singer, "no external data" (the bank's readme); its Spanish phonemes are listed in Lotte V's [Phoneme_chart.md](https://github.com/lottev1991/LotteV-Voicebank-Update-Repo/blob/main/Phoneme_chart.md). It is the only licence-clean Spanish DiffSinger voice found (VOICES.md, "Spanish": the search).
- **Measured** (examples/vocals/svs/spanish.py; five lines, dry, Whisper small multilingual): **96% word accuracy** for both voices (27 of 28 words; the miss: "guardo" heard as "cuarto", a word-initial /gw/ heard voiceless), Whisper's language detector hears Spanish on 5 of 5 lines (`hanami-es`) and 4 of 5 (`hanami-es-soft`, one line read as Italian at p 0.41). The same lines through the English path (cmudict) score 4%. The owner's ear has not judged the accent yet; 7 minutes of data can leave a slight foreign colour.
- **No Spanish TTS path.** Kokoro v1.0 has Spanish voices (`ef_dora`, `em_alex`, `em_santa` in `voices-v1.0.bin`), but `engine/tts_words.py` speaks English only (eSpeak `en-us`/`en-gb` phonemes, English segmentation rules). Not wired, and per the owner's decision for session 014 not needed: the svs voice passed.

## How the engine reads Spanish (`engine/lang_es.py`)
Spanish spelling is close to phonemic, so a short rule set (a G2P) gives the bank's phonemes; the bank's own Spanish dictionary (`dsdict-es.yaml`, MIT) wins where it lists a word (numbers, names, consonantal "y" words). Checked against that dictionary: 269 of 278 Spanish words identical; the rest are loanwords and typos in the dictionary.
- **Variety: Latin American** (Lipski 1994, *Latin American Spanish*, Longman): **seseo** (c before e/i and z are /s/: "corazón" ko-ra-son, "cielo" sye-lo) and **yeísmo** ("ll" and consonantal "y" are one sound, the bank's `j`: "lleva" je-bha). Castilian /θ/ (`th`) is in the bank but not used; Caribbean /s/-aspiration and final-/s/ loss (bachata's Dominican Spanish) are not modelled: write the full spelling.
- **Five vowels, the same in stressed and unstressed syllables:** /i e a o u/ (`i e a o u1`), "which may occur in both stressed and unstressed syllables" ([Martínez-Celdrán, Fernández-Planas & Carrera-Sabaté 2003, JIPA 33(2), 255-259](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0025100303001373)). No reduction to schwa, unlike English: every written vowel is sung full. Good for singing: a Spanish vowel can be held on any syllable.
- **Glides and diphthongs:** an unstressed i/u next to another vowel is a glide in the same syllable: before the vowel `y`/`w` ("quiero" kye-ro, "cuando" kwan-do, "nueve"), after it `I1`/`U1` ("seis", "causa", "hoy"); an accent on í/ú breaks the diphthong ("aquí", "día" di-a, "río"); two weak vowels: the second carries the syllable ("ciudad" syu-dhadh, "muy", "fui"). Silent u in qu, gue, gui; ü sounds ("pingüino").
- **b, d, g are stops only after a pause or a nasal, d also after l; "in other positions they are pronounced as their approximant variants"** [β ð ɣ] (Martínez-Celdrán et al. 2003). The engine applies this inside words and across words within a sung phrase ("la vida" la-bhi-dha; after a rest "vida" starts with a stop). b and v are the same sound.
- **Tap and trill:** "the trill appears in word onset and after [l], [n] and [s] and in intervocalic position where it contrasts with the tap" (rr: "perro" vs "pero"); the tap elsewhere (Martínez-Celdrán et al. 2003). The bank: `rr` and `rx`.
- **Unaspirated p, t, k** (`px tx kx`), clear l (`l1`), ñ (`ny`), ch, j/ge/gi as /x/ (`x`), h silent, n becomes m before b, v, p, f ("un beso", "enfermo").
- **Syllables:** one consonant between vowels starts the next syllable ("pa-la-bra"); of two, the second does, except stop or f plus l or r, which start a syllable together ("es-cri-bo", "pa-la-bra", "ha-blar"); glides stay with their vowel. Write lyrics hyphenated by these syllables (`co-ra-zón`, `pa-la-bra`); the engine checks the count and warns.

## Stress and rhythm
- **Lexical stress, mostly on the penultimate:** "Most Spanish words bear the stress on the penultimate syllable but any of the three final syllables may be stressed" (Martínez-Celdrán et al. 2003); the written accent marks the exceptions (corazón, aquí, mañana is regular). In a song the stressed syllable belongs on a strong beat or a longer or higher note; a stressed syllable on a weak 8th and an unstressed one on the downbeat sounds wrong to a native ear (the same principle as English text setting; GENERAL.md "Stress"). The svs voice has no stress symbol: the notes alone make the stress, so place it.
- **Syllable-timed rhythm:** Spanish groups with the "syllable-timed" languages (with Italian, French, Catalan), with a larger share of vocalic time (%V) and less variable consonant intervals (ΔC) than English, Dutch or Polish ([Ramus, Nespor & Mehler 1999, Cognition 73(3), 265-292](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0010-0277(99)00058-X)). Syllables come at a steady rate with full vowels, so even 8th-note syllables are natural in Spanish where English wants long notes on content words; Latin pop melodies run many syllables on 8ths and 16ths and stretch only the phrase-final, stressed one.
- **Phrase endings:** most words end in a vowel or s, n, l, r, d; open endings let a line ring on its last vowel.

## Linking (sinalefa)
- **Vowels across words merge into one syllable** (*sinalefa*, synalepha: "mi amigo", "te escribo" te-es → tes): a native monolingual group linked such vowels 93% of the time in a narrative task ([Willis, Holt & Carver 2025, Linguistik Online 134(2), 27-50](https://doi.org/10.13092/lo.134.12177)); Spanish verse counts syllables after synalepha by rule (metrical scansion; e.g. [Automatic Scansion of Spanish Poetry, arXiv 2012.12799](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.12799)), and lyricists set songs the same way.
- **In the engine:** one syllable per note, so to sing a sinalefa write the merged syllable on one note, spelled as one word ("tes-cri-bo" for "te escribo" on three notes) or give each vowel its own note (the hiatus, slower, more deliberate: tested, "te es-cri-bo" on four notes was transcribed correctly). Whether the model resyllabifies consonant-vowel joins across words ("el amor" as e-la-mor) has not been measured.

## Writing Spanish lines for Spectre (practical)
- Short lines, concrete words, one idea; the voice is an instrument (standing rule 14): a refrain fragment, a call, a few words answered by the requinto, not verses.
- Register as the other Hanami voices: A3-D5, sweet spot F#4-B4; `hanami-es-soft` thins above C5.
- Long notes on open stressed vowels (a, o, e); a final -s or -n closes the note: put it at the end of the note, not as a new note.
- Check every line with Whisper after rendering (examples/vocals/svs/spanish.py shows how), and keep the lyric in the journal.
