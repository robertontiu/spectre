# Voice: mixing and mastering (all genres)

How to put a synthesized voice into a mix and through the master so that it sounds right and its words survive. This file holds what is true in every genre, the research on choirs and ensembles, and how to check a voice by measurement. Each genre doc has its own "Mixing the voice" section with a starting chain:
[caroling](../CAROLING.md#mixing-the-voice-researched-2026-11-24), [trance](../TRANCE.md#mixing-the-voice-researched-2026-11-24), [Latin pop](../LATIN_POP.md#mixing-the-voice-researched-2026-11-24), [pop rock](../POP_ROCK.md#mixing-the-voice-researched-2026-11-24), [hip hop](../HIPHOP.md#mixing-the-voice-researched-2026-11-24), [joc and Romanian folk](../JOC_TIGANESC.md#mixing-the-voice-researched-2026-11-24).

How the voice itself works (speech, singing, timing, intelligibility of sung words, vibrato) is in [GENERAL.md](GENERAL.md); which voice to pick is in [VOICES.md](VOICES.md); the controls are in [docs/MANUAL.md](../MANUAL.md) "Voices".

**How to read it.** A value with a source is what engineers or measurements report. A value marked *(hand-tuned)* is Spectre's starting point and has no source. Published interviews say what engineers do far more often than they give settings, and for several of Spectre's genres no account with numbers exists; the genre sections say where. The owner's listening notes are symptoms to diagnose with these methods, not prescriptions: a note like "too much reverb" is tested (see the session 012 diagnosis in CAROLING.md, where the reverb turned out to be one of two causes).

## 1. Principles

### Words: what makes them survive
- **A sung word is fragile before any mixing.** Sung words alone, unaccompanied, were misheard 7 times as often as the same words spoken (26% against 3.6%), and 78% of the errors were consonants ([Collister & Huron 2008](https://d-scholarship.pitt.edu/19444/1/EMR000050a%2DCollister%2DHuron.pdf)). On records, listeners catch about 72% of the words on first hearing (GENERAL.md, "Intelligibility of sung words"). A mix can only lose more; the aim is that the few exposed words read.
- **The mix loses words in three ways:** something else is louder in the same band (masking), something fills the gaps that consonants are made of (reverb, delay, other voices out of time), or the consonants themselves are removed (a low-pass filter, a dark voice). Each has its own fix, and the check in section 6 tells them apart.
- **Musicians name the same factors:** diction, the balance between singers and accompaniment, and the room were the most cited in a survey of 143 musicians ([Fine & Ginsborg 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00809/pdf)).

### Level against the band
- **Measured on records:** the lead vocal sits 1-2 dB over the accompaniment in chart music since about 1975 (it was 5 dB in 1946); by genre country 3.9 dB, rap 3.2, pop 2.7, rock 0.2, metal -3.1; solo artists 1.5-3.5 dB higher than bands ([Gerdes & Siedenburg 2023](https://www.geidai.ac.jp/~marui/course_enshu_2024/gerdes_siedenburg_202304.pdf); A-weighted levels of separated stems, error about 1-1.6 dB). These are leads carrying a whole song.
- **For Spectre** the voice is an instrument (standing rule 15), so those figures are ceilings. Spectre measures in loudness units (section 6), which is close to but not the same as the paper's A-weighted dB. A line whose words matter: the genre's lead level or up to 1 LU under it. A texture: 4-10 LU under.
- **Make room before adding level.** Engineers cut the band where the voice needs to be heard: 1-2 kHz out of guitars or keyboards, or parts panned wider ([Ken Andrews, Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/inside-track-paramore)); "only use the sounds in the frequency ranges you want them to be active in" ([Armin van Buuren, Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/people/armin-van-buuren)). In the blend study 2.5 dB less 2.5 kHz in the guitars did more than level (MANUAL "Voices").

### Tone: body, presence, air
- **High-pass** as high as it goes before the tone changes (preamp filters sit at 80 Hz; 100 Hz tames plosives; stacked backing voices 150-200 Hz) ([Paul White, Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/vocal-production); [Mike Senior, Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/mixing-pop-vocals)).
- **Cut low mids before boosting highs:** "A deep reduction at 500Hz is equal to a huge gain at 2kHz and above" (Tony Maserati, quoted in Vocal Production); documented cuts: 3 dB at 250-300 Hz, 4 dB shelving from 450 Hz ([Tingen](https://tingen.org/making-of-bieber-despacito-remix/); [Sound On Sound, Bodak Yellow](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/inside-track-cardi-b-bodak-yellow); [Mix Rescue](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/mix-rescue-jay-menon)).
- **Presence** 2.5-4.5 kHz, gently, with a moderate Q; a documented lead used +2 dB at 4.5 kHz ([Craig Anderton, Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/how-make-your-vocals-twice-good-part-2); Tingen). Heavy 3-6 kHz boosts sound harsh (Senior, in Vocal Production).
- **Air:** a touch of high shelf at 8 kHz (White). Every dB of it raises sibilance and breath as well (section "Compression and sibilance").

### Space: reverb, pre-delay, distance
- **Distance is the ratio of direct to reverberant sound** (how choirs are placed in front of microphones: CAROLING.md). A close voice has little reverb and hears it late.
- **Pre-delay keeps the voice in front of its reverb:** from a barely noticeable 30-40 ms to over 100 ms, like a nearby voice in a large room whose reflections arrive later (White). Documented: 50 ms on a 3.5 s hall, 16 ms on a plate (Mix Rescue), 15 ms on a 7 s effect hall (Bodak Yellow).
- **How much:** pull the reverb return down until the voice comes apart from the backing, then set it between that point and where you started (White). Reverb should be subtle, "ideally only the loudest musical peaks should cause obvious tails", and two seconds is usually long enough ([Sound On Sound, Reverb: first principles](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/reverb-first-principles)).
- **Filter the reverb, not the voice:** roll the reverb off below about 150-200 Hz and above 3-4 kHz for intimacy (White; Mix Rescue). De-ess what is sent to reverbs and delays, because effects "draw unwanted attention to esses" ([Matt Houghton, Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/managing-sibilance)).
- **Short ambience glues, long tails decorate:** an ambience under 100 ms with little pre-delay locks a voice into a band; halls and delays are ridden per section and often thrown on single words (Mix Rescue; Vocal Production; Tingen).
- **Reverb costs consonants.** It fills the silence before a plosive and covers the sibilant after a vowel (GENERAL.md cites Vurma et al. 2025). Measured here: at the "s" of "Peace" the vowel band dipped 29-33 dB dry and only 19 dB with sends of 0.3 into a 1.15 s reverb, and removing the reverb brought back three of eight words in two different passages (CAROLING.md, diagnosis).
- **Delay** for thickening is an 8th note; for space a quarter note with three or four repeats; slap about 100 ms with no feedback (Anderton; White). A repeat that lands on the next word masks it: long delays go on phrase ends.

### Compression and sibilance
- **Moderate on a natural voice:** 2:1 to 4:1, attack about 10 ms (20-30 ms keeps consonant transients), release 50-100 ms, no more than about 5-6 dB of gain reduction; level rides first, compression second (White; Anderton). Documented leads: ratio 3 at threshold -12 followed by a second stage at about 2:1 (Tingen); 6:1 on a dance-pop lead ([Sound On Sound, Clean Bandit](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/inside-track-clean-bandit-solo?page=3)); "quite heavily" in trance ([Darren Tate, Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/secrets-house-trance)).
- **Compression and brightening raise sibilance and breath.** Sibilant energy is strongest at 3-6 kHz and reaches past 10 kHz, higher for female voices; de-essing comes early in the chain, and settings have to be revisited after EQ and compression (Houghton). Documented de-essers work at 4.3-4.4 kHz (Bodak Yellow) and at 7 and 14 kHz (Tingen); one a cappella mix used two in series so neither worked hard ([Sound On Sound, Pentatonix](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/inside-track-pentatonix)).
- **The engine has no de-esser** (section 5). Until it has one: keep high shelves at 3-4 dB, low-pass the reverb and delay buses, and never stack shelves to reach a master curve.

### Doubles and width
- **A double is a second performance:** a copy moved a few milliseconds and 5-7 cents sounds like one (White); real doubles are panned left and right to clear the centre for the lead (Pentatonix); a widened copy of a pop lead sat 20 dB under it (Tingen). Doubling thickens and costs punch (TRANCE.md, MusicRadar tips).
- **In the engine** `double` is a real second take (its own pitch curve), `double_cents` 7 and `double_ms` 12 by default. A lead: `double` 0.1-0.25 panned 0.7. Don't use `double` on voices that already have partners on other tracks.

### Chops, lines, choirs: three different jobs
| | a line with words | a chop | a choir or pad texture |
|---|---|---|---|
| what it is | text: a hook, a call, a refrain fragment | rhythm and colour made of voice | harmony and space made of voices |
| must the words read? | yes | no (state the words once as a line first if they matter) | only if it sings a text; see section 3 |
| note length | a quarter note or more per syllable that matters (MANUAL) | any | long |
| voice | the clear ones (`hanami`, `hanami-power`, `hanami-es`) | the bright, hard ones | any, the dark and soft ones included |
| reverb | little, late (pre-delay 40-100 ms) | by taste; delay for bounce | much |
| timing | exact; one breath before the first word | gated to the grid | scattered, unless it sings words |
| compression | 3:1 to 4:1 | heavy | none or light |
| level | the genre's lead level or just under | by groove | 4-10 LU under the lead element |
| filter sweeps | never across words | free | free |

## 2. What a synthesized voice needs that a recorded one doesn't
A DiffSinger voice arrives already "produced" in some ways and unfinished in others. Measured on Spectre's banks (examples/vocals/svs/README.md "Blend", VOICES.md, and the diagnoses in the genre docs):
- **No microphone, no room, no proximity.** There is nothing to high-pass away except the model's own low end, and no space at all: the dry voice against a band sounds like "someone singing along to a speaker". It needs a room given to it (a small late reverb), where a recorded voice often needs its room hidden.
- **The default treatment (since 2026-11-26, the owner's choice by ear: blend-d).** A new project sets `"voice_defaults": 2` and every `singer` voice gets the blend-d chain unless it sets its own (docs/MANUAL.md "`voice_defaults`"): `hpf` 100, 800 Hz -2.5 dB, 3 kHz -3 dB, a +3 dB shelf at 8 kHz, a 3:1 `comp` at -22 dB, `send_reverb` 0.32 behind a 60 ms `reverb_predelay_ms`, `send_delay` 0.12, a `double` of 0.2 panned 0.7, `inhale` 0.3, phrase fades of 30 / 120 ms; a choir voice (`"role": "choir"`) gets `svs_vibrato` 0.3 instead (chosen over 1: `examples/vocals/svs/choir-vibrato-0.3.mp3`). The 2.5 kHz dip in the band that blend-d also used is a mix move, made on the band per track, not part of the voice default. The points below are why.
- **Dull on top and narrow.** Against a guitar band the voice was 3.8 dB short at 8 kHz, 7-8 dB heavy at 500 Hz-1 kHz (EQ moves this by about 1 dB only), and more mono than the band (L/R coherence 0.77 against 0.52). Hence the standing chain: `hpf` 100, a small dip near 800 Hz, +3 dB shelf at 8 kHz, a quiet wide double.
- **Already even in level.** The model sings at a steady effort; there are no shouted peaks to control, so compression is for density and style, not for safety. The banks have no breathiness, tension or energy controls (VOICES.md): "intimate" and "belted" are choices of voice mode, not of processing.
- **Phrase edges are abrupt** (a 90-10 % phrase release of 160 ms in the blend study): `svs_fade_in_ms` 20-30, `svs_fade_out_ms` 100-140 and `inhale` 0.3 make a phrase start and end like breath.
- **Consonants are where the model put them and no longer:** the vowel lands on the note, the consonants come before it, and scaling consonant lengths did not help a machine listener (GENERAL.md, section 5). The mix cannot add diction; it can only avoid removing it. A word tied to the note before it has no onset at all: give every worded phrase a rest before its first word.
- **Dark voices are blurred voices.** `hanami-dark` (formants lowered) is misheard dry and alone; in session 012 it sang the quoted words an octave under a clear soprano, louder than her, and took the line with it. Words go on the clear voices; dark and soft voices sing vowels.
- **Every take is seeded.** A different `seed` is a different performance of pitch and timbre noise, which is what makes real doubles and choirs possible (section 3), and `svs_vibrato` narrows vibrato per voice.
- **Register is hard-edged.** Outside A3-D5 the voice fails instead of straining (VOICES.md); high /i/ and /u/ lose identity as they do for sopranos (GENERAL.md).
- **Neural artefacts sit in the top octave** (vocoder noise, breath). A shelf at 13 kHz boosts those and not the voice; session 012's +11 dB there bought hiss and a phone-check flag, not clarity.

## 3. Choir and ensemble voices
The engine and the standing rules treat a choir texture as one more "voice as an instrument", next to chops and hook lines. The research says a choir is a different thing to mix, to master and to control, because its sound is made by the differences between its singers. This section is that research and what follows for svs choirs. Genre sections that use choirs (caroling first; trance pads) refer here.

### What the research says
- **The choral sound comes from scatter.** A unison section is never in unison: pitch scatter among section singers is about 10-15 cents (standard deviation) in rehearsal (Ternström & Sundberg 1988, in GENERAL.md), and the beating among many slightly different voices is the "chorus effect". Listeners tolerate about 14 cents of scatter, but most prefer none ([Ternström, Choir acoustics: an overview](https://scispace.com/pdf/choir-acoustics-an-overview-of-scientific-research-published-2em0kyqmrt.pdf)). So: enough difference to be several people, not enough to be out of tune.
- **Choral mode is a different voice use.** The same singers put more energy at 2-4 kHz when singing solo and more in the fundamental when singing in a choir; solo vibrato is wider; listeners "overwhelmingly" preferred the blended choral mode to a strong singer's formant (Rossing et al. 1986, 1987 and Ford 1999, in Ternström's overview). A vocal quartet adjusts vibrato for blend (Daffern 2017, in GENERAL.md).
- **Blend is also a level relation.** Each singer hears their own voice 3-4 dB over the rest of the choir on average (+1 to +8 dB by position), and prefers about +6 dB (Ternström 1995, 1999, in the overview). That is a singer's ear inside the choir; for the listener it means no voice should stand out of its section, and a voice that does is a soloist.
- **A few singers are enough.** The characteristic choral sound needs "less than a handful" of voices per part; each doubling of singers adds 3 dB, and beyond about 60 there is little to gain ([Titze, The economy of choir size](https://vocology.utah.edu/_resources/documents/the_economy_of_choir_size_titze.pdf)).
- **Timing scatter is audible and costs words.** Two singers started notes 57 ms apart on average with eye contact (95 ms without); a quintet stayed around 49 ms across five rehearsals; listeners notice differences somewhere between 10 and 38 ms ([D'Amario, Daffern & Bailes 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/148848/1/journal.pone.0218162.pdf); sung legato on one vowel, so consonants were not studied). Several singers are less intelligible than one, mostly in the final consonants (Condit-Schultz & Huron 2017, in GENERAL.md).
- **Tuning is not equal temperament by default,** but close: preferred major thirds averaged 395 cents against 400 tempered and 386 pure (Nordmark & Ternström 1996, in the overview). The engine's tempered notes are inside what choirs do.
- **Spacing and seating:** singers and listeners prefer wider spacing between singers; sectional seating was preferred to mixed in one study and made no difference in another (Daugherty 1996, 2001; Tocheff 1990, in the overview).
- **Room:** singers want early reflections 15-35 ms after the direct sound, dislike a reflection at 40 ms, and care more about how loud the reverberation is than how long (Marshall & Meyer 1985, in the overview); singers preferred a 1.77 s room to a 4.79 s one and to a dry one, slowed down as the room grew, and kept time best dry ([Fischinger, Frieler & Louhivuori 2015](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstream/123456789/49954/1/influenceofroomacousticsonchoirsinging.pdf)).
- **Recording and mixing a choir** (CAROLING.md has the microphone practice): a main pair for the whole, section spots three to four feet away for definition, spots panned slightly narrower than the pair, high-pass at 85-110 Hz, a room made of early reflections with no pre-delay plus a hall tail of 2.66 s ([Sound On Sound, Session Notes](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/session-notes-recording-choir)). A remote-ensemble production put the same high-pass, compressor and de-esser on every singer, timed the group to one hand-edited voice, used a short chorused ambience and a longer hall together, and controlled harshness and sibilance of the whole choir with a multiband compressor on the mix bus ([Mike Senior, cambridge-mt.com resources for the Sound On Sound article](https://cambridge-mt.com/misc/sos-remote-vocal-ensemble-production)). The a cappella pop tradition tunes every voice tightly, de-esses twice, pans doubles left and right, and widens choir tracks away from the centre (Pentatonix).
- **A choir under a band:** no source with levels or settings was found. Practice in the sources above: stacked backing voices are high-passed at 150-200 Hz (White), darkened and squashed to stay under a lead, and sent to a larger reverb of their own (a church preset; Pentatonix).
- **Mastering a choir:** choral records are quiet and dynamic: Christmas choral albums measure about -19 to -23 LUFS (CAROLING.md), and "something in the range of -18 to -12 LUFS is likely to sound just fine", with a little compression or limiting allowed ([Ian Stewart, iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/mastering-vocals)). Choir sibilance and harshness are handled on the whole choir, with multiband compression (Senior, above).

### What this means for svs choirs
- **Separate takes, not copies.** One track per singer, each with its own `seed` (its own pitch curve and timbre noise), and where possible another mode or bank (Root, Nectar, LEONA). This is what `double` does for one voice; a choir does it per part. A copy shifted in time or pitch is a chorus effect, not a choir.
- **Two to three voices per part are enough** ("less than a handful"); a fourth adds 1-2 dB and render time. A choir is mostly upper voices: two or three sopranos, two altos; tenor and bass go to `hanami-male` (VOICES.md "Male voices"; soft and covered, not tried as a choir yet) or to instruments (CAROLING.md).
- **`svs_vibrato` 0.3-0.5 on every choir voice** (choral mode has narrower vibrato; measured in GENERAL.md: 22.7 cents of fast pitch movement at 1, 16.0 at 0.5). Leave one voice at 0.7-1 only if it is meant to be the soloist.
- **Pitch scatter comes for free** from separate seeds; don't add detune on top unless a measurement shows the section tighter than about 5 cents. The preferred amount is small.
- **Timing scatter: yes on vowels, no on words.** For "oo" and "ah" textures, `push_ms` spread over about ±10-25 ms per voice makes several people (the measured human spread is larger, around 50 ms, but that was judged as asynchrony). For a line with words, every voice at `push_ms` 0 and `humanize_ms` 0: in session 012, four voices starting "Peace" up to 36 ms apart lost the word, and the same voices aligned kept all eight words (CAROLING.md).
- **Words on all the voices or on none.** GENERAL.md suggests keeping the words on one voice while the others sing vowels. Tested on session 012's four-voice line with the other three voices on "ah" at their mix levels, that lost the words (1 of 8): the vowels mask the single text. It can only work with the vowel voices well under the worded voice (not tested); the reliable ways to thin a worded line are fewer voices, or the same words on all of them in time.
- **No soloist by accident.** Voices of a part within about 3 dB of each other; the tune on top 2-3 dB over the lower parts. Clear voices lead; `hanami-dark` and the soft voices fill.
- **Choral tone is duller than solo tone.** No presence boost and no air shelf on texture voices (less 2-4 kHz is the choral mode); only the worded line gets the 8 kHz shelf.
- **Panning and seating:** a small group in a line, sections together: sopranos left of centre, altos right, each singer at their own position (for example -0.5, -0.25, +0.25, +0.5), partners of a part not on the same spot; a pad choir wider (±0.4-0.8). Wide spacing is preferred, mixed seating is not needed.
- **One room for all of them.** The same `send_reverb` and `reverb_predelay_ms` for every voice of the choir, more than a lead would get: a hall at `size` 0.6-0.75 (1.4-2.1 s) with sends of 0.2-0.35 for a texture; a worded choir closer (0.1-0.2). The engine's reverb has no separate early reflections: the `room` effect supplies them but smeared a single voice's words in the blend study, so use it on wordless choirs only *(hand-tuned)*.
- **Under a band:** `hpf` 200-250 Hz, a dip of 3-5 dB at 2.5-3 kHz (the band the lead or the word line needs), sends 0.5-0.7 into the genre's reverb, 6-10 LU under the lead element, and in club music a deep kick sidechain so the choir breathes between the hits *(hand-tuned; session 013's choir is built this way)*.
- **Control guidance the engine lacks** (recommendations, section 5): a choir is many tracks whose settings must stay equal; today each is edited by hand.

## 4. Mastering and the voice
- **What a master does to a voice.** It changes the tone of everything at once, and it reduces the level difference between loud and quiet. The first moves the voice's presence and sibilance along with every other instrument in those bands; the second brings up whatever sits between and behind the syllables: reverb tails, delays, breaths, the band. High-frequency boosts on the master "naturally bring up any sibilance" (Houghton). Mastering processing can make sibilance worse, and de-essing there is "not ideal": it belongs in the mix; when it is done at mastering it is a band at 5-10 kHz at no more than 2:1, or a mid-channel cut of no more than 1.5-2 dB ([Sage Audio](https://www.sageaudio.com/articles/de-essing-during-mastering)).
- **Vocal level is a mix decision.** "There's no one number I can give you here", and rebalancing a vocal at mastering "shouldn't be a replacement for changing things in the mix when needed" (Stewart, iZotope). One mix engineer keeps a bus compressor on the whole time so that the balance is made through it and not changed afterwards (Clean Bandit).
- **Small speakers:** "the midrange carries better over small speakers", and a voice that gets lost there is helped at 1-2 kHz (3-6 dB on the affected notes), not at the top ([Mike Senior, Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/node/4927107)); the classic check is one small mono midrange speaker (Senior, Mixing Pop Vocals).
- **Loudness:** the louder the genre's target, the more the limiter and glue act between syllables. Spectre's targets run from -14 (caroling) to -9 (trance); the limiter works 1-2 dB at the loud end. Judge words on the master in the loud genres.

### What Spectre's master does to a voice
`engine/master.py` (docs/MASTERING.md) applies one static 1/3-octave EQ to the whole track, moved in a closed loop toward the genre's target curve (at most +4 dB per band, +2 dB from 5 kHz up, cuts to -8), then dynamics, loudness and a true-peak limiter. It reads only the premaster; it does not know there is a voice.
- **The curve decides how bright the voice ends up, and a borrowed curve can decide wrongly.** `caroling`, `joc` and `latinpop` reuse the `rock` curve. In session 012 the producer, reading the trial master's "dull at 5-12 kHz", added +11 dB shelves at 4.7 and 13 kHz to every voice, because voices were the only tracks with energy there. The curve was satisfied and the voices hissed. A master report that asks for top end is a question about the arrangement (what should be up there: bells, cymbals, strings) before it is a request to brighten the voice.
- **The EQ is fitted to the whole track,** so sections with a voice get the correction that the sections without one needed. Session 009's master asked for more 125-160 Hz once the voice added weight at 500 Hz-1 kHz.
- **The phone check** (`listen --phone`, presence peak at 3 kHz) reacts to consonants and inhales: in session 012 it flagged the two inhales and the "P" of "Peace" over a filtered organ. That is a level and filter problem in the mix.

### Recommendations for the engine (not implemented; for the owner to decide)
1. **A `deess` effect in `apply_fx`** (a split-band compressor above about 5 kHz, with a threshold relative to the track's level), usable on a voice and, more important, on what a voice sends to the reverb and delay buses. This is the largest gap against every documented vocal chain.
2. **A voice check as a tool** (`tools/voxcheck.py` or `studio.py listen --words`): for every `singer` track with lyrics, render with the voices muted, report per section the vocal-versus-band loudness and the wet-to-dry ratio, and transcribe each sung line with Whisper on the mix, on the voices alone and on the master. Sessions 009, 012 and 013 each wrote their own script for this.
3. **Voice-aware notes in the master report,** without changing the processing: when the project has `singer` tracks, report the master EQ's gain at 2-5 kHz and 5-10 kHz and warn when it boosts either by more than about 2 dB ("the master is brightening the voice: fix the mix"), and report the limiter's gain reduction during the sung bars.
4. **Measured curves for the voice-led acoustic genres** (choral, folk, Latin), so that their masters stop asking a choir for a rock record's top end. Until then `--eq` 0.5 or less for caroling.
5. **Ducking of the reverb and delay returns from a chosen track** (the engine's `sidechain` listens to drum tracks only), so that long trance tails can stay loud between words and get out of the way under them.
6. **A second reverb bus** (or per-track decay), so a close worded voice and a far choir can share a mix without sharing a room; today the choice is one `size` for everything or an insert `reverb`.
7. **Choir control:** a way to declare several `singer` tracks one section (shared fx, sends and vibrato; per-voice seed, pan and timing offset generated), so equal settings stay equal.
8. **No voice-specific processing inside `master` itself** (no mid-channel de-esser, no vocal rebalance): the sources agree those are repairs for mixes that cannot be reopened, and Spectre can always reopen the mix.

## 5. The engine's controls, measured
- **Sends:** `send_reverb`, `reverb_predelay_ms` (a per-track delay before the reverb bus), `send_delay`; one reverb bus and one delay bus per project (`mix.reverb`: `size`, `damping`, `return_db`, `lpf`; `mix.delay`: `beats`, `feedback`, `return_db`, `lpf`; the reverb bus is high-passed at 250 Hz, the delay bus at 300 Hz).
- **Reverb decay by `size`** (impulse through the bus, T30; `damping` 0.4-0.7 changes it by under 3%): 0.15 = 0.7 s, 0.25 = 0.8 s, 0.35 = 0.9 s, 0.5 = 1.15 s, 0.6 = 1.4 s, 0.75 = 2.1 s (default), 0.85 = 3.0 s, 0.9 = 4.0 s, 0.95 = 5.5 s. The reverb's own first reflection arrives about 25 ms after the sound, before any `reverb_predelay_ms`. It cannot be shorter than about 0.7 s.
- **Wet against dry for a voice** (blend study, session 009's bus): send 0.22 = reverb 11.4 dB under the dry voice, send 0.32 = 8.4 dB under; the guitar band in the same mix sat at 22 dB under. Each halving of the send is 6 dB.
- **Insert effects** (`fx`, in order): `hpf`, `lpf`, `eq` (bell), `lowshelf`, `highshelf`, `comp` (threshold, ratio, attack, release; no make-up gain: end the chain with `gain`), `drive`, `sat`, `delay` (for a slap: `{"type": "delay", "beats": 0.25, "feedback": 0, "mix": 0.1}`), `reverb` (an insert, for a private space), `chorus`, `width`, `gate`, `room` (early reflections; smears words), `pitch`.
- **Singer controls that are mix decisions:** `double`, `double_pan`, `double_cents`, `double_ms`, `inhale`, `svs_fade_in_ms`, `svs_fade_out_ms`, `svs_vibrato`, `seed`, `gender`; track `pan`, `width`, `push_ms`, `humanize_ms`, `sidechain` (from a drum track), section `auto` (`gain_db`, `lpf`, `hpf`, `mute`).
- **Missing:** de-esser, ducking of returns, a second reverb, multiband compression, per-word automation (a word that needs its own effect goes on its own track).

## 6. Checking a voice
Two measurements, both from renders the session already makes. They are proxies: Whisper is one run of one model and its count moves by a word between runs, and loudness says nothing about tone. Use them to tell causes apart and to compare versions, then let the owner's ear decide.

**Vocal against band.** Render the project (`vNNN.premaster.wav`), then render it again with every voice track on `"mute": true` in a scratch copy. The voice as heard in the mix, with its reverb and delay, is the first premaster minus the second (rendering is deterministic, so everything else cancels); the second is the band. Per section with a voice: integrated loudness of the voice minus that of the band (pyloudnorm), in LU. A `--solo` render is not the same thing: it leaves out the returns. Rendering both again with the returns off (`return_db` -200) gives the wet-to-dry ratio as well (`examples/vocals/svs/blend.py` does all four).

**Words.** Cut the bars of each sung line (half a second before to half a second after), transcribe with faster-whisper `small.en` (`small` forced to the language for Spanish), and count the line's words found in order:
```python
import os; os.environ.setdefault("HF_HOME", os.path.expanduser("~/.cache/spectre/svs/hf"))  # where tools/setup.sh installs the models
from faster_whisper import WhisperModel
m = WhisperModel("small.en", device="cpu", compute_type="int8", cpu_threads=2)
segs, _ = m.transcribe(x16k_mono, language="en", beam_size=5, condition_on_previous_text=False)
```
(`sessions/013-decay/words.py` is a complete per-line script for a master.) Run it three ways and read the pattern:

| voices alone | mix | master | the problem is |
|---|---|---|---|
| reads | reads | reads | nothing |
| reads | reads | fails | the master: loudness target, or its EQ; check the report |
| reads | fails | | masking or space: level against the band, room in the band, reverb and delay sends, timing against other voices |
| fails | | | the voice itself: the voice chosen, register, note lengths, a filter on the track, no rest before the first word, voices out of time with each other. No mix move fixes this |

Chops and wordless textures are not expected to transcribe. A dense guitar or supersaw mix reads 50-60% at best even when a listener follows it (session 009), so compare versions, not absolute numbers.
