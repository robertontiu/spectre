## Speech to song: what changes

### Timing: what lands on the beat (measured on human singers)
- **Perceived onset.** The perceptual centre (P-centre) of a syllable is its "subjectively perceived moment of occurrence"; it "seems to lag behind the acoustic onset" and is "mostly assumed to lie somewhere close to the vowel onset", shifted mainly by the onset consonants; one model puts it at the fastest energy rise of the consonant-vowel transition ("maxD", Šturm & Volín) ([Rathcke, "The P-Center Effect and the Domain of Beat Perception in Speech", in Meyer & Strauss (eds.), Rhythms of Speech and Language, Cambridge UP 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/B2C7ECDB04F16EE327A38445D2C8EFF7/core-reader); the classic studies are Morton, Marcus & Frankish 1976 and [Marcus 1981, Perception & Psychophysics 30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7322800), not read in full here).
- **How much timing error is noticeable.** In an isochronous sequence the just noticeable displacement is about 6 ms for inter-onset intervals under 240 ms and about 2.5% of the interval above (10.7 ms at 500 ms, 21 ms at 800 ms) ([Friberg & Sundberg 1995, JASA 98](https://scispace.com/pdf/time-discrimination-in-a-monotonic-isochronous-sequence-ynslrz5yhj.pdf)). `tools/joincheck.py --timing` flags onsets beyond it.
- **What human singers put on the beat** (`tools/humanref.py`, GTSinger: 3 English singers, 300 phrases of plain singing, 3,245 one-note syllables; [Zhang et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13832), [data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/GTSinger/GTSinger), CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, measured only, nothing copied): fitting each phrase's score grid (the notes' score durations) to the audio, the syllable start (its first consonant) fits best (median residual 31 ms) and the vowel onset worst (54 ms), with a steady trend between; the same for each singer and at slow, moderate and fast pace. Against the grid the consonants start on the beat (median +1 ms) and the vowel follows about 90-106 ms later; the maxD P-centre lands about 120 ms after the beat. This contradicts the classical rule of consonants anticipated before the beat (choral singing: Hauck 2020; HMM synthesis: Saino et al. 2006; VOCALOID puts the vowel on the note), which Spectre had followed; for pop singing in this corpus, the word starts on the beat. Caveat: in GTSinger's annotation every note starts where its word starts; if annotators drew word boundaries partly from the notes, the fit would favour word starts. The score durations used for the grid are independent of those boundaries, and the result holds for every singer and pace, but the annotation process was not checked here.
- **How long singers make the consonants** (same corpus, sung vs the same singer reading the same words): onset consonants median 81 ms (one consonant), 160 ms (two), 215 ms (three); sung onset ≈ 59 ms + 0.54 x spoken onset (short onsets lengthen, long ones shorten); codas keep their spoken length (median ratio 1.00); vowels 2.3x their spoken length; the vowel takes about 60% of a 200-800 ms note and 78% of a longer one; the consonant lead hardly depends on the time available (80-90 ms at any gap).
- **In the engine:** the timing analysis of the "Here You Are" chorus showed the vowels on the grid but the words with long onsets ("here", "round", "hold", "stay") starting 75-150 ms (up to 0.3 beat) early, because the whole onset was placed before the note: the "rushed in" words, while vowel-initial words kept cadence. `onset_beat` (1.0) starts the onset on the note (the share of the onset sung after the note start, at most half the note) and `onset_fit` lengthens onsets to the singers' rule; a vowel-initial word's "onset" in a phrase is the previous word's linking consonant, so it stays before the note (the singers have no lead there). Against the GTSinger phrases sung by the engine, before -> after (singers): consonant start vs beat -44 -> -12 ms (+1), vowel -1 -> +72 ms (+106), lead 35 -> 78 ms (91), vowel share 0.88 -> 0.82 (0.81), maxD P-centre +6 -> +85 ms (+123). On the chorus the spread of perceived onsets against the score fell from 50 to 40 ms, the inter-onset error from 72 to 61 ms and the words beyond 3x the timing JND from 8 to 5; on the dry "here you are, round and round" the spread fell from 52 to 35 ms.

### Timing: the classical rule (consonants before the beat)
- Singers start consonants early: "human singing voice has a tendency to start consonants a little earlier than the timing of musical note", so the HMM singing synthesizer of Saino et al. models a time-lag per note that can be negative ([Saino, Zen, Nankaku, Lee & Tokuda, Interspeech 2006](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2006/saino06_interspeech.pdf)). In choral recordings, initial consonants are "nearly always anticipated" before their beat, with exceptions for some plosives and the second consonant of a cluster, and the anticipation depends on how lengthenable the consonant is ([Hauck 2020, Music Performance Research 10](https://boris.unibe.ch/149910)).
- VOCALOID places the vowel onset "strictly on the NoteOn position", with the consonant material before it ([Kenmochi & Ohshita, Interspeech 2007](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2007/kenmochi07_interspeech.pdf)).
- In the engine this was the rule until the GTSinger measurements above (`"onset_beat": 0`); `keep_joins` (2026-11-24) ends a syllable exactly where the next onset starts, so the consonants at a join keep their spoken length instead of being overwritten.

### Sustaining: stretch the steady vowel, keep the transitions
- "Unlike speaking, we sustain vowels for singing ... the vowel shape must be held for the duration of the tone"; in a diphthong the emphasis falls on the first vowel ([K-State Vocal Techniques, "Articulation"](https://kstatelibraries.pressbooks.pub/vocal-techniques/chapter/articulation/)).
- Concatenative singing synthesis keeps phoneme transitions as recorded (diphone samples) and builds the sustained vowel from stored sustained-vowel samples, interpolating spectral envelopes ([Kenmochi & Ohshita 2007](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2007/kenmochi07_interspeech.pdf); background: [Bonada & Serra, IEEE Signal Processing Magazine 24, 2007](https://mtg.upf.edu/node/512)). Statistical synthesis distributes extra note length across model states by their variance (Saino et al. 2006), i.e. mostly into the variable (steady) parts.
- In the engine: `stretch: "core"` keeps the first and last `trans_ms` (40) of each vowel at spoken speed and stretches only the steady core (lingering on its first part, as for the first element of a diphthong). Measured on the "Here You Are" chorus: the next vowel's first 30 ms were played 2.5-9.5x slower than spoken before (mean |log2 warp| 2.28), 1.0x on most joins after (mean 0.71).

### Pitch at a join
- Note transitions sit near note boundaries; data-driven singing F0 models align them to within tens of ms of the note onset ([Hua, "Modeling Singing F0 With Neural Network Driven Transition-Sustain Models"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.04030)).
- Measured: with the pitch move starting at the onset consonant, 75-100% of it happened after the next vowel had begun (a slide inside the vowel, heard as forced). Since the vowel should start on its note (VOCALOID's rule above), the engine now starts the move earlier (`glide_early` 0.8 of the transition time), so most of it passes through the consonants: on the chorus, the share inside the vowel fell from 0.80 to about 0.3 on average.

### Stress: let the melody set the emphasis
- Stress in speech is cued by duration, intensity, pitch and spectral balance; in Campbell's measurements "relative energy in the higher spectral regions proved the best correlate of prominence" (energy 2-8 kHz), loudness at the fundamental the weakest ([Campbell, ICPhS 1995](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/Phonetics/icphs/ICPhS1995/13_ICPhS_1995_Vol_3/p13.3_676.pdf)).
- In a song the note placement and length carry the emphasis; a spoken phrase's own stress pattern sung over a melody puts accents where the music has none (Robert's "each 'round' seems emphasised").
- Measured: the engine already evens vowel loudness per syllable, but the spoken spectral tilt survived intact (correlation spoken/sung 0.97, spread 7.0 dB across the chorus's syllables). `prosody` (0.7) moves each syllable's tilt and onset force toward the same syllable spoken alone (its citation form) and leaves spoken pitch accents out of `micro`: the tilt spread fell to 4.9 dB and the onset-force spread from 3.2 to 2.8 dB. A vowel-initial word's onset in a phrase is the previous word's linking consonant, which its citation form lacks, so it is not corrected.

### Speaking rate of the sampled phrase
- Singers keep consonants short and put them before the beat (above); slower TTS speech mainly lengthens consonants (Kokoro at speed 0.8 instead of 0.9: the /h/ of "here" 135 -> 215 ms), so the engine keeps `tts_speed` 0.9 and gets vowel length from the notes.
