# Voice: general knowledge (all languages)

What applies to every language Spectre sings in: how speech is made and timed, what singing changes, how singing synthesizers handle it, and how a voice sits in a band. Language specifics live in [languages/](languages/) (`en.md`, `es.md`, `ro.md`); the voices themselves in [VOICES.md](VOICES.md); the engine parameters in docs/MANUAL.md, "Voices". Standing rule 10 (docs/ORCHESTRATION.md) asks for peer-reviewed sources where they exist: each section says what the evidence is and how strong it is, and each engine choice names the finding it rests on.

The engine sings words with **`svs`** (`singer`, DiffSinger voicebanks): a model trained on a singer sets phoneme durations, the pitch curve (scoops, transitions, vibrato) and the timbre. The engine chooses the notes, the syllables, the phoneme symbols, the seed and a few controls; it cannot move what the model learned, only frame it. So most of what follows is guidance for writing lines (where stresses, long notes and rests go), for choosing the phonemes, and for mixing, and the yardstick for judging a voice.

Voices are instruments (standing rule 14): short lines, hooks, calls, refrain fragments and choir textures, not songs. Several findings matter more for that role than for a lead vocal (intelligibility of a few exposed words, blend in a texture, level under the band).

**Researched 2026-11-24** (queue item 5b): a literature pass over JASA, Journal of Voice, JIPA, Journal of Phonetics, Interspeech/ICASSP and music-perception journals; DOIs were checked against Crossref and findings against the abstracts (PubMed, OpenAlex, publishers). Where only an abstract was read, the text says so.

## 1. Speech: production and prosody

### How a voice is made
- **Source and filter.** The larynx makes a buzz (the source: its pitch is F0, its spectrum falls about 12 dB per octave in modal voice); the vocal tract filters it, and its resonances (formants F1, F2, F3...) make the vowels. F1 rises as the jaw opens (/a/ high, /i/ /u/ low), F2 as the tongue moves forward (/i/ high, /u/ low). Classic reference: Fant, *Acoustic Theory of Speech Production* (Mouton, 1960). For a sung voice, pitch and vowel are set separately, which is what lets a DiffSinger bank sing any vowel on any note, within limits (section 2, "High notes").
- **Vocoders split them the same way:** WORLD analyses speech into F0, a spectral envelope (the filter) and aperiodicity (breath, noise), and resynthesizes from them ([Morise, Yokomori & Ozawa 2016, IEICE Trans. Inf. & Syst. E99-D(7), 1877-1884, doi:10.1587/transinf.2015EDP7457](https://doi.org/10.1587/transinf.2015EDP7457)); Spectre uses its analysis to measure voices (spectral tilt, breathiness, vibrato in VOICES.md).

### Stress: which cues make a syllable prominent
- In English, **duration** and **intensity** both cue stress, duration the stronger of the two in Fry's classic perception experiments ([Fry 1955, JASA 27, 765-768, doi:10.1121/1.1908022](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1908022); pitch movement, tested in later work, is stronger still where an accent falls).
- Overall loudness is a poor cue, but **spectral balance** is a good one: stressed vowels have more energy above about 0.5 kHz, because more vocal effort raises the high partials more than the low ones; "Duration proved the most reliable correlate of stress ... Spectral balance, however, turned out to be a reliable cue, close in strength to duration" ([Sluijter & van Heuven 1996, JASA 100, 2471-2485, doi:10.1121/1.417955](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.417955); Dutch, with the same pattern in American English). Campbell found the same for prominence (energy 2-8 kHz the best correlate; [Campbell, ICPhS 1995](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/Phonetics/icphs/ICPhS1995/13_ICPhS_1995_Vol_3/p13.3_676.pdf)).
- **What this means sung:** the melody now sets duration and pitch, so the remaining speech stress cue is spectral tilt. A spoken phrase's tilt pattern carried into a song puts accents where the music has none. DiffSinger banks get no stress symbols at all (the dictionaries strip them): the notes make the stress, so stressed syllables must be written onto strong beats, longer or higher notes (section 2, "Text to melody").

### Rhythm: stress-timed, syllable-timed and in between
- Languages differ in how much of speech is vowel and how variable the consonant stretches are: English, Dutch and Polish have a smaller vocalic share (%V) and more variable consonant intervals (ΔC) than Spanish, Italian, French or Catalan ([Ramus, Nespor & Mehler 1999, Cognition 73(3), 265-292, doi:10.1016/S0010-0277(99)00058-X](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0010-0277(99)00058-X)); the pairwise variability of successive vowel durations (nPVI) separates them too, with many languages in between rather than in two clean classes ([Grabe & Low 2002, in *Laboratory Phonology 7*, 515-546, doi:10.1515/9783110197105.515](https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110197105.515)). The classes are tendencies built from syllable structure and vowel reduction, not isochrony: stress-timed languages have complex syllables and reduce unstressed vowels ([Dauer 1983, Journal of Phonetics 11, 51-62, doi:10.1016/S0095-4470(19)30776-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0095-4470(19)30776-4)).
- **For writing lines:** a syllable-timed language (Spanish) sits naturally on runs of even 8ths; a stress-timed one (English) wants the content words on long or strong notes and lets function words pass quickly; Romanian is mixed (languages/ro.md). Each language profile says where it sits.

### Phrase-final lengthening
- Durations carry structure: in English, segment duration is a primary cue to vowel length, stress, voicing of a following consonant, emphasis and to **phrase-final vs non-final** position ([Klatt 1976, JASA 59, 1208-1221, doi:10.1121/1.380986](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.380986)).
- The lengthening before a prosodic boundary is "restricted to the rhyme of the syllable preceding the boundary" (the vowel and what follows it, not the onset or earlier syllables), and its size grades with the boundary's strength (four distinguishable degrees) ([Wightman, Shattuck-Hufnagel, Ostendorf & Price 1992, JASA 91, 1707-1717, doi:10.1121/1.402450](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.402450)). Later work found it can also reach the stressed syllable of a phrase-final word when the stress is not final ([Turk & Shattuck-Hufnagel 2007, Journal of Phonetics 35, 445-472, doi:10.1016/j.wocn.2006.12.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wocn.2006.12.001); title and venue checked, abstract not available here).
- In music the analogue is the slowing at phrase ends and the final ritardando: performers lengthen notes at phrase boundaries ([Repp 1992, JASA 92, 2546-2568, doi:10.1121/1.404425](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.404425)), and final ritardandi follow the tempo curve of a runner stopping at constant braking power ([Friberg & Sundberg 1999, JASA 105, 1469-1484, doi:10.1121/1.426687](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.426687)); the KTH performance rules model both ([Friberg, Bresin & Sundberg 2006, Advances in Cognitive Psychology 2, 145-161, doi:10.2478/v10053-008-0052-x](https://doi.org/10.2478/v10053-008-0052-x)).
- **For Spectre:** the band keeps tempo, so a sung line does its phrase-final lengthening in the notes: write the last stressed syllable of a phrase on its longest note (a held note, or one tied over the bar), and let the line end into a rest. The engine does not stretch time on its own (mind/hand split: phrasing is a musical decision).

### Coarticulation and connected speech
- Neighbouring sounds overlap: in vowel-consonant-vowel sequences the transitions into and out of the consonant depend on both vowels, so the tongue plans across the consonant ([Öhman 1966, JASA 39, 151-168, doi:10.1121/1.1909864](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1909864)). Words in a phrase link (a final consonant becomes the next word's onset, vowels merge across words in Spanish and Romanian), and function words shrink.
- **For the engine:** a DiffSinger bank models coarticulation itself (its acoustic model sees the whole phoneme sequence), so the engine only has to give it the right phonemes in phrase order, including cross-word effects the dictionary can't know (Spanish b/d/g softening across words; Romanian likewise needs phrase-level rules, languages/ro.md).

## 2. Singing

### Text to melody: where the stresses go
- In songs, composers put linguistic stress on metrical accent: in Gilbert and Sullivan and in poetry set to music, compound nouns took the stronger beat on their first syllable (43 vs 5 and 96 vs 9 cases) and adjective-noun phrases on the second (79 vs 16), following the English compound and nuclear stress rules; and singers lengthened syllables that carried either linguistic or musical accent, the two effects independent ([Palmer & Kelly 1992, Journal of Memory and Language 31, 525-542, doi:10.1016/0749-596X(92)90027-U](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596X(92)90027-U)).
- **Rule for writing lines:** put each word's stressed syllable on a beat at least as strong as its neighbours', or on a longer or higher note; in a phrase, the main stress (usually the last content word) on the strongest position. A mismatch is heard as a wrong word, more so in a language with a fixed or predictable stress (Spanish, Romanian) than in one where the listener expects it to move. The singer does not know the meter, so this is the producer's job (MANUAL "Voices").

### Timing: what lands on the beat (the evidence, reconciled)
The question matters because DiffSinger puts the vowel on the note, while a measurement of human singers (GTSinger, below) seemed to put the consonant on it.

**Evidence for the vowel (or the perceived onset) on the beat:**
- **Accompanied art song, commercial recordings:** "most commonly, the accompanists synchronized their tones with the singers' vowel onsets", with expressive leads and lags that were smallest at a fast tempo and largest at a slow one ([Sundberg & Bauer-Huppmann 2007, Journal of Voice 21(3), 285-293, doi:10.1016/j.jvoice.2006.01.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2006.01.003); abstract read).
- **Choirs:** initial consonants are "nearly always anticipated" before their beat, with exceptions for some plosives and the second consonant of a cluster, and more anticipation for consonants that can be lengthened ([Hauck 2020, Music Performance Research 10](https://boris.unibe.ch/149910)).
- **Perception:** the moment a syllable seems to occur (its P-centre) lies later than its acoustic start, close to the vowel onset, and later the longer and more complex the onset: measured against a noise burst, "ba" 7 ms, "la" 42, "pa" 53, "sa" 106, "spa" 184 ms ([Villing, Repp, Ward & Timoney 2011, Attention, Perception & Psychophysics 73, 1614-1629, doi:10.3758/s13414-011-0110-1](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-011-0110-1); a pilot adjustment by one author, reported in the paper). For musical sounds, a slower attack and a longer duration both move the perceived moment later and blur it ([Danielsen et al. 2019, J. Exp. Psychology: Human Perception and Performance 45(3), 402-418, doi:10.1037/xhp0000611](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000611)). The classic studies: Morton, Marcus & Frankish 1976; [Marcus 1981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7322800); review: Rathcke 2026 (Cambridge UP, *Rhythms of Speech and Language*).
- **Synthesis practice:** HMM singing synthesis models a time-lag between the note and the phoneme because singers start consonants early ([Saino, Zen, Nankaku, Lee & Tokuda, Interspeech 2006](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2006/saino06_interspeech.pdf)); Sinsy and NNSVS keep a separate time-lag model ([Nakamura et al., ICASSP 2020, doi:10.1109/ICASSP40776.2020.9053811](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICASSP40776.2020.9053811); [Yamamoto, Yoneyama & Toda, ICASSP 2023, doi:10.1109/ICASSP49357.2023.10096239](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICASSP49357.2023.10096239)); VOCALOID places the vowel onset "strictly on the NoteOn position" ([Kenmochi & Ohshita, Interspeech 2007](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2007/kenmochi07_interspeech.pdf)); DiffSinger datasets are labelled so that "each word should start with a vowel/AP/SP, and end with leading consonant(s) of the next word", because "in singing, vowels, instead of consonants, are used to align with the beginnings of notes" (OpenVPI's MakeDiffSinger dataset guide, [ph_num README](https://huggingface.co/ameerazam08/MakeDiffSinger/blob/main/variance-temp-solution/README.md)). That is a labelling convention of the data, so it says what the model reproduces, not what singers do.

**Evidence for the consonant on the beat:** the GTSinger measurement (`tools/humanref.py`; 3 English singers, 300 phrases, 3,245 one-note syllables; [Zhang et al., NeurIPS 2024, doi:10.52202/079017-0034](https://doi.org/10.52202/079017-0034), [data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/GTSinger/GTSinger), CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, measured only): fitting each phrase's score grid to the audio, the syllable start fit best (median residual 31 ms) and the vowel onset worst (54 ms), with a steady trend between, for every singer and pace; the maxD P-centre (fastest 300-3000 Hz energy rise, Šturm & Volín) lands about 120 ms after the fitted grid.

**How long human singers make the parts of a syllable** (the same GTSinger measurement, sung vs the same singer reading the same words): onset consonants median 81 ms (one consonant), 160 ms (two), 215 ms (three); sung onset ≈ 59 ms + 0.54 x spoken onset (short onsets lengthen, long ones shorten); codas keep their spoken length (median ratio 1.00); vowels 2.3x their spoken length; the vowel takes about 60% of a 200-800 ms note and 78% of a longer one; the consonant lead hardly depends on the time available (80-90 ms at any gap). A yardstick for a voice's consonant and vowel lengths.

**Reconciling them:**
1. **The GTSinger result measures regularity, not phase.** The grid is fitted per phrase with a free offset (and tempo), and the recordings are a cappella: there is no beat outside the singer. "Consonants start on the beat (median +1 ms)" is therefore true by construction of the fit (whichever anchor is chosen lands at about 0); the informative part is only that word starts are spaced more evenly than vowel onsets. That is compatible with singers who time their words from the word start, and also with an annotation in which note boundaries were drawn at word boundaries (GTSinger notes start where words start), which would favour word starts.
2. **With an accompaniment, the evidence says the vowel or the P-centre is on the beat** (accompanists follow vowel onsets; choirs anticipate consonants; the perceived moment of a syllable is near its vowel onset). A voice in a band is heard against the drums, so this is the case Spectre is in.
3. **The two agree on durations.** Both find sung onset consonants of about 60-100 ms (GTSinger: 81 ms median for one consonant; DiffSinger: 63-102 ms leads, README in examples/vocals/svs/), so the practical difference is where a 60-100 ms window sits: before the beat, or after it.
4. **The JND brackets what matters:** timing errors become noticeable at about 6 ms for fast note rates and 2.5% of the interval above 240 ms ([Friberg & Sundberg 1995, JASA 98](https://scispace.com/pdf/time-discrimination-in-a-monotonic-isochronous-sequence-ynslrz5yhj.pdf)); ensemble asynchronies are noticed above roughly 10-38 ms ([D'Amario, Daffern & Bailes 2019, PLoS ONE 14(6), e0218162, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0218162](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0218162)). A 60-100 ms shift of the vowel is far above both, so the choice is audible.
5. **Genre caveat:** across 88,357 popular-music vocal tracks (1965-2010), rap vocal onsets arrive later than the beat on average while other genres tend to anticipate it, more so on beats 1 and 3, a tendency that has moved closer to the grid over time ([Georgieva et al. 2026, TISMIR 9(1), 179-193, doi:10.5334/tismir.278](https://doi.org/10.5334/tismir.278); abstract read, the onset landmark not checked).

**Practice:** keep the model's vowel-on-the-note timing (no change): the voice is heard against a band, where the evidence puts the vowel or the P-centre on the beat. A rule that shifts the consonant onto the beat would rest on the a cappella fit alone.

### Intelligibility of sung words
- **How much listeners get:** for commercial recordings heard once, lyric intelligibility averaged 72%, from 48% (classical) to 96% (jazz) across twelve genres; it rose modestly on a second and third hearing ([Condit-Schultz & Huron 2015, Music Perception 32(5), 470-483, doi:10.1525/mp.2015.32.5.470](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2015.32.5.470)). So a sung line in a band is not expected to be fully understood; a few exposed words should be.
- **Several voices:** four singers in sync are less intelligible than one, but more singers (up to 27) cost little more; the loss is mostly in **coda consonants** (the plosives /b d g p/ are easily confused, liquids too), consistent with conductors' attention to word endings ([Condit-Schultz & Huron 2017, Journal of Voice 31(1), 121.e1-e8, doi:10.1016/j.jvoice.2016.02.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2016.02.011)). For choir textures: keep exposed words' endings simple (open vowels, nasals), Don't put the words on one voice while the others sing vowels over it: in an svs test that lost the words (1 of 8 heard; docs/voice/MIXING.md, choir section). Words in all voices of the part, starting together (`push_ms` 0), is what read.
- **Consonant length, room and accompaniment:** at speaking pitch, dry and alone, sung voiced consonants (/m n l v/) of 20-35 ms were recognised almost perfectly; with reverberation, longer consonants let the vowel's reverb decay and helped; with an accompaniment-like noise, consonants of about 200 ms or more helped; higher pitch and longer reverb hurt ([Vurma et al. 2025, JASA 158, 3120-3132, doi:10.1121/10.0039581](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0039581); abstract read). Tested on Spectre's svs voice (section 5, item 4): scaling the model's consonant durations did not help a machine listener in noise, so the engine leaves them as the model predicts, but the reverb finding stands: reverb on the words masks the consonants that follow (the blend study's early reflections killed Whisper's transcription; section 4).
- **What singers and listeners think matters:** in a survey of 143 musicians, diction (performer), hearing (listener), acoustics (room) and genre were the most cited factors ([Fine & Ginsborg 2014, Frontiers in Psychology 5, 809, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00809](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00809)).

### High notes: vowels lose their identity
- When F0 rises above a vowel's spoken F1, there is no harmonic left to show F1. Sopranos then **tune the first resonance to the pitch** (R1 ≈ F0), and R2 follows, so "vowels move, converge, and overlap their positions on the vocal plane ... to an extent that implies loss of intelligibility" ([Joliveau, Smith & Wolfe 2004, JASA 116, 2434-2439, doi:10.1121/1.1791717](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1791717); also [Nature 427, 116, doi:10.1038/427116a](https://doi.org/10.1038/427116a)). Across voice types: sopranos use R1:F0 tuning in their upper range for all vowels, altos for low-R1 vowels, tenors and baritones tune R1 to the 2nd or 3rd harmonic ([Henrich, Smith & Wolfe 2011, JASA 129, 1024-1035, doi:10.1121/1.3518766](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3518766)).
- Listeners hear high sung vowels shift "toward vowels with an F1 just above the sung frequency" ([Hollien, Mendes-Schwartz & Nielsen 2000, Journal of Voice 14(2), 287-298, doi:10.1016/S0892-1997(00)80038-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0892-1997(00)80038-7)).
- **For Spectre:** words are clearest where F0 stays below most vowels' F1 (about A3-D5 for the female voices; the singer warns outside a voice's range). On high notes write open vowels (/a/, /o/, /e/, whose F1 is highest) and expect /i/ and /u/ to drift toward them; an important word belongs below about C5. The svs model was trained on a real singer and does its own vowel modification inside its trained range; outside it (the voice's range in VOICES.md) it fails.

### Register, the singer's formant and pop voice quality
- Classical (operatic) voices have a **singer's formant**, a peak around 2.5-3 kHz made by clustering F3-F5, which carries the voice over an orchestra; Sundberg explained it by a lowered larynx and a narrow tube above the vocal folds ([Sundberg 1974, JASA 55, 838-844, doi:10.1121/1.1914609](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1914609)).
- Non-classical styles work differently: one professional singer's rock, pop, soul and Swedish dance-band styles differed in formant frequencies and in subglottal pressure (rock and dance band at opposite extremes) ([Borch & Sundberg 2011, Journal of Voice 25(5), 532-537, doi:10.1016/j.jvoice.2010.07.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2010.07.014); abstract read). Pop singing relies on the microphone and the mix, not on a singer's formant, for projection.
- **For Spectre:** don't add a 3 kHz "singer's formant" boost to the pop voices; carving 2.5-3 kHz in the guitars and adding air at 8 kHz worked better (section 4). The svs banks' own modes (Root, Fragrance/power, Nectar/soft) are the register and effort controls they have (VOICES.md).

### Vibrato and pitch scatter
- **Classical vibrato:** rate about 6 Hz on average (ten singers, Schubert's Ave Maria on CD), rising by about 15% toward the end of each tone ([Prame 1994, JASA 96, 1979-1984, doi:10.1121/1.410141](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.410141)); extent ±34 to ±123 cents per tone, ±71 cents on average, smaller on longer tones ([Prame 1997, JASA 102, 616-621, doi:10.1121/1.419735](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.419735)). Pop vibrato is shallower and later; the svs voices measure 18-39 cents sd on long notes (VOICES.md), within that frame.
- **Ensembles and blend:** pitch scatter between section singers in rehearsal was 0.10-0.15 semitone (10-15 cents) sd ([Ternström & Sundberg 1988, JASA 84, 59-69, doi:10.1121/1.396875](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.396875)); for unison choir sounds, listeners preferred 0 or 5 cents of scatter and tolerated about 14 ([Ternström 1993, Journal of Voice 7(2), 129-135, doi:10.1016/S0892-1997(05)80342-X](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0892-1997(05)80342-X)); a vocal quartet adjusted its vibrato for blend, with some synchrony between parts ([Daffern 2017, Journal of Voice 31(3), 385.e23-e29, doi:10.1016/j.jvoice.2016.09.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2016.09.007); a systematic review: [Acosta Martínez & Daffern 2025, Journal of Voice 39, doi:10.1016/j.jvoice.2022.11.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2022.11.004)).
- **For the engine:** `double_cents` (7) sits between the preferred and the tolerated scatter; separate takes already have separate pitch curves. `svs_vibrato` (section 5, item 2) scales the svs pitch model's vibrato for straighter choir and pad textures.

### Sustaining and legato
- Sung vowels are held where speech moves on: "the vowel shape must be held for the duration of the tone", and in a diphthong the first vowel carries the length ([K-State Vocal Techniques, "Articulation"](https://kstatelibraries.pressbooks.pub/vocal-techniques/chapter/articulation/); a teaching text, not peer-reviewed). Measured in GTSinger (section 2, "Timing"): sung vowels were 2.3x their spoken length and took about 60-80% of a note, while codas kept their spoken length.
- Synthesis keeps the transitions and stretches the steady part: concatenative systems keep recorded transitions (diphones) and build the sustain from stored sustained vowels ([Kenmochi & Ohshita 2007](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2007/kenmochi07_interspeech.pdf); [Bonada & Serra 2007, IEEE Signal Processing Magazine 24(2), 67-79, doi:10.1109/MSP.2007.323266](https://doi.org/10.1109/MSP.2007.323266)); statistical synthesis spreads extra length across model states by their variance (Saino et al. 2006). DiffSinger's duration model does it itself.

## 4. A voice in a band: level, space and masking

- **Lead-vocal level in records:** the level of the lead vocal against the accompaniment (A-weighted, from separated stems) fell from about +5 dB to +1 dB between 1946 and 1975 and stayed there; by genre (1990-2020 Grammy nominees) country +3.9 dB, rap +3.2, pop +2.7, rock +0.2, metal -3.1; solo artists 1.5-3.5 dB above bands ([Gerdes & Siedenburg 2023, JASA Express Letters 3(4), 043201, doi:10.1121/10.0017773](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0017773)).
- **For a voice used as an instrument** (standing rule 14), sit below those lead levels: the approved svs chorus works about 1 LU under the instrumental (examples/vocals/svs/README.md, "Blend", variant d), roughly the rock-lead level; a texture or pad lower still; a hook that must be understood, at most the genre's lead level.
- **Space without smear:** reverb from a vowel masks the consonant after it (Vurma et al. 2025, section 2); early reflections on the voice made it blend but Whisper lost the words, while a reverb send behind a 60 ms pre-delay gave space and the best word accuracy (62% on the full master vs 42%; blend study). Keep the words dry-ish and put the space behind them.
- **Spectrum:** the svs voice sat 3.8 dB short of the band at 8 kHz and 7-8 dB heavy at 500 Hz-1 kHz (blend study): hpf about 100 Hz, a small dip near 800 Hz, a high shelf for air, and room carved at 2.5-3 kHz in guitars or saws rather than a presence boost on the voice (and no singer's-formant boost, section 2).
- **Width:** the svs voice is more mono than a band (L/R coherence 0.77 vs 0.52); a quieter, wider double (0.2, panned 0.7) matched the band's width without blurring words.
- The mix chain that came out of this is the svs example in docs/MANUAL.md "Voices".

## 5. Engine improvements for the `svs` path (prioritized, 2026-11-24)

| # | improvement | source | status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Keep DiffSinger's vowel-on-the-note timing; no consonant-on-beat shift for svs | Sundberg & Bauer-Huppmann 2007; Hauck 2020; Villing et al. 2011; the DiffSinger labelling convention | decided: no change (section 2, "Timing") |
| 2 | `svs_vibrato` 0-1+: scale the pitch model's vibrato and fast pitch movement inside held notes (transitions kept), for straight-tone choir textures, pads and tight doubles | Ternström 1993; Ternström & Sundberg 1988; Daffern 2017; Prame 1997 | **implemented, opt-in** (default 1 = bit-identical); measured below |
| 3 | Mix defaults for a voice as an instrument: level under the genre's lead level, reverb behind a pre-delay, no early reflections on words, carve the band at 2.5-3 kHz, air at 8 kHz | Gerdes & Siedenburg 2023; Vurma et al. 2025; the blend study | documented (MANUAL "Voices" example, section 4); no engine default changed (mixing is the producer's decision) |
| 4 | Scale consonant durations for diction in a dense mix | Vurma et al. 2025 | **tested and rejected**: no gain (below) |

**Item 2, measured** (`python3 examples/vocals/svs/vibrato.py measure`; six lines, Hanami Root): fast pitch movement inside held vowels (the audio's pitch minus its 205 ms moving average, inside each vowel's phoneme span less 60 ms at each end) **22.7 cents sd at `svs_vibrato` 1, 16.0 at 0.5, 11.7 at 0**; on the pitch curve the vocoder is fed, 23.8 -> 8.1 cents at 0 (the rest is slower movement and the 50 ms ramp-in at each note). The vocoder's output follows the edited curve to within 0.6-0.8 cents sd. Whisper (small.en) on the dry lines: 92-94% of words at every setting (no cost to diction). Defaults verified bit-identical against the previous `svs.py` on empty phrase caches. A/B: `examples/vocals/svs/choir-vibrato-1.mp3` vs `choir-vibrato-0.3.mp3` (three takes: hanami, hanami-soft a third below, leona-soft); the blend needs Robert's ear.

**Item 4, measured** (six lines, Hanami Root; consonant durations scaled before the note-span alignment, so the vowel gives up the time; Whisper small.en; pink noise as a band stand-in): consonants 93 ms mean at 1.0, 125 ms at 1.6, 73 ms at 0.7. Word accuracy at SNR -5 / -10 dB: 1.0: 57% / 24%; 1.6: 53% / 6%; 0.7: 56% / 19% (brown noise, as in Vurma et al., masked too little to separate them: 92-97% at -5 dB). The model's own durations are as good as any scaling, so the control was removed again. Caveats: one machine listener, 36 words per condition, one noise seed per line; humans in Vurma et al. benefited from much longer voiced consonants (about 200 ms) against noise.
