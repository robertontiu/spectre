## 1. Layering
**The principle.** What a listener hears as one sound is usually several sources playing the same notes, each supplying something the others lack: weight, body, bite, air, the attack, the width. Classical orchestration does it by doubling (the same line on several instruments, in unison or at the octave), rock by double-tracking and by miking one instrument several ways, hip hop by stacking drum hits and splitting a bass into sub and harmonics, trance by stacking synths. The research on recorded music sees the result from outside: popular music since the 1970s is measurably more inharmonic than orchestral or piano music, and Deruty, Meredith and Lattner (2023) name multitracking and studio layering, many simultaneous tones at close frequencies, as the main reason [S].

**Roles** (the vocabulary used in every genre doc; frequency ranges are typical, the genre EQ maps give the actual ones):
| Role | What it supplies | Typical range | Typical source |
|---|---|---|---|
| sub | weight you feel | 30-90 Hz | a sine, an 808, a bass drum's shell, organ pedal, double bass |
| body | the note's size and warmth | 90-400 Hz | the main oscillator or instrument, low strings, guitar bodies |
| mid bite | what small speakers play; the pitch you follow | 700 Hz-4 kHz | saws, driven layers, bow noise, pick attack, consonants |
| top / air | openness, breath | 8-14 kHz | a thin bright layer, noise, cymbals, bell partials, room |
| transient | where the note starts; how close it feels | the first 5-30 ms, mostly above 1 kHz | a pluck, a click, a second shorter hit, a hammer |
| width | size | above about 200-300 Hz only | a wide double, detuned sides, a different take left and right |
| centre | focus and mono safety | | a narrow or mono layer at pitch |

**Rules that hold across the sources** [S]:
- *Contrast, not copies.* "Successful layering is all about contrast" (Native Instruments). Two layers that do the same job make a clogged midrange, not a bigger sound (MusicRadar). Choose each layer for the thing the first one lacks (NI; van Buuren).
- *One layer owns each role.* Only one layer carries the transient; supporting layers get slower attacks and longer releases (NI). Only the sub lives below about 150 Hz (Cold Rush). "Only use the sounds in the frequency ranges you want them to be active in" (van Buuren, Sound On Sound 2009).
- *Main layer centred, support wide* (NI); wet against dry keeps two layers apart even in one range (Sound On Sound 2004).
- *EQ the stack together,* not each layer in solo (NI); filters shift phase, so layers of one sound are checked summed (Sound On Sound 2022).
- *Glue:* group the layers on a bus and treat them as one instrument: gentle compression (2:1, 2-4 dB), a little saturation, shared reverb and sidechain (Attack Magazine; MusicRadar).
- *Less is more* has its advocates too: one large lead with reverb and delay instead of a stack (Ferry Corsten, MusicRadar 2017). The aim is that every role is filled once, not a layer count.

## 2. EQ carving and masking: richer or muddier?
**What mud is.** A sound is *masked* when other sounds in the same band at the same moment are loud enough that the ear no longer resolves it. A mix reads as rich when many sources are each still audible somewhere; it reads as muddy when many sources share a band and none wins, most often in the low mids, where nearly every instrument has energy (boxiness and mud at 200-500 Hz appear for kick, bass, piano, strings, acoustic guitar and room microphones alike in iZotope's EQ cheat sheet) [S]. Low frequencies also use most of a mix's headroom, so low-end clutter is the first EQ target (Mike Senior, Sound On Sound 2008) [S].

**What the research measures** [S]:
- *Masker-to-signal ratio per band.* For each track, compare its energy in each auditory band with the masking threshold produced by the sum of all other tracks (the MPEG psychoacoustic model; bands on the Bark scale); a track's masking value sums the bands where it lies under that threshold. Ronan, Ma, Mc Namara, Gunes and Reiss used it to drive EQ and compression automatically; in their listening test (24 listeners) the raw summed mixes were never rated clearer than the automatically de-masked ones, while human mixes still beat the automatic ones in nearly all cases.
- *Masked-to-unmasked ratio* (Aichinger, Sontacchi and Schneider-Stickler, AES 2011): an instrument's loudness in the mix as a share of its loudness alone. Instruments at 10% or less were "critical": most listeners could not identify them adequately.
- *Partial loudness* (Ma, Reiss and Black, "Partial loudness in multitrack mixing", as cited by Ronan et al.; not read for this brief): the loudness of a track in the presence of the others, from a loudness model. The principled measure, and the heaviest to compute.
- *A caution from the same group.* Hafezi and Reiss (JAES 2015) built an automatic equalizer on a simplified masking measure; it reduced perceived and measured masking and came close to a professional's mix, but they conclude that existing objective measures are poorly suited to quantifying *perceived* masking in music. Numbers find the candidates; ears confirm.
- *EQ is not the only tool, and maybe not the best.* In Wakefield and Dewey's test (AES 138, 2015) of mirrored EQ, spectrum sharing by low-pass and high-pass, and panning, participants preferred the tools that panned the two tracks. Panning does nothing in mono (Houghton, Sound On Sound 2022), so both are needed.

**How producers carve** [S], [P]:
1. *Arrangement first.* Change the notes, the octave or the sound, or make another part less busy; mute what fights (Houghton, Sound On Sound 2022). If something sounds muddy the cause is often the arrangement or the balance, not the frequency content (Thinkspace).
2. *High-pass what doesn't need lows:* a 70 Hz, 12 dB/octave filter on most sources except kick and bass, gentler and higher filters to move others out of their way (Houghton); "the innocent hi-hat samples in packs can contain unwanted low frequencies" (Bjorn Akesson, via EDMProd).
3. *Decide who owns a band, cut the other there.* Build the mix in order of importance and cut each new track where it masks the earlier ones; cuts get more severe as tracks get less important (Senior). Reciprocal EQ: "a smaller boost to the primary sound, and a smaller cut to the secondary one" (Houghton). The trance producer's EQ map [P] is this, written out for a whole genre: for every instrument a high-pass point, a region to cut, a region it owns (TRANCE.md).
4. *Carve in time as well as in frequency:* sidechain ducking from the kick; better, a dynamic EQ cut keyed from the competing source, which does "less collateral damage" (Houghton); in hip hop a dynamic notch on the 808 at the kick's fundamental (iZotope); shaping envelopes so onsets don't coincide (Houghton; NI).
5. *Treat the effects as sources:* high-pass and low-pass reverb and delay returns and duck them under their own source (Houghton; Attack Magazine; [P]).
6. *Mid/side:* clear the centre of supporting stereo sources and leave their sides (Houghton; NI).
7. *Where not to carve.* In orchestral and acoustic ensemble music, giving each instrument its own frequency slot "breaks up the natural blend of sections"; EQ should be "gentle and broad"; spacing and balance in the orchestration do the job (Thinkspace). A guide for sampled orchestras allows broad 1-2 dB cuts at 200-500 Hz on section buses and a roll-off under violins around 200 Hz, no more (Beat Kitchen). The genre docs for joc, caroling and chamber score follow this, not the trance map.
8. *"There are no specific frequencies that will always work"* (Senior). Every EQ map in these docs is a starting range, to be confirmed on the actual sounds by the analysis below and by ear.

**What Spectre can and cannot see today** [M: MANUAL, ear.py]. `listen` shows the mix: lanes, a mel spectrogram, five band energies, loudness, onsets, width, warnings; `--phone` checks the top end against the curve. Per-track information is only an RMS envelope (`stems_rms`). Nothing compares stems with each other, so "is the pad audible under the lead", "how many things live at 300 Hz" and "which layer supplies the air" are invisible, and sessions wrote their own helper scripts (`stems.py`, `bands.py`, `peaks.py`) again and again to guess at it.

### A clarity analysis for `listen` (proposal, prototyped)
The engine renders every track separately before summing, so the stems cost nothing extra. Prototype (a throwaway script, 2026-11-25; 14 s for an 8-bar section with 12 tracks on this machine): render one section's stems in memory, add the reverb and delay returns as two more stems, take each stem's power in 28 third-octave bands (31.5 Hz-16 kHz) per 93 ms frame, and print:
1. **Layer census.** Per region (sub 20-60, bass 60-150, low-mid 150-400, mid 400 Hz-2 kHz, presence 2-6 kHz, air 6-18 kHz): the region's share of the mix, and the stems within 10 dB of the loudest stem there. An empty or single-source region is a missing role; five sources within 10 dB in the low-mid is a crowd.
2. **Masked share per stem.** The share of a stem's A-weighted energy lying in time-frequency cells where all other stems together are at least 6 dB above it; and its **own share**, in cells where it leads the others. A simplified, time-resolved masker-to-signal ratio; the 6 dB margin and the A-weighting are hand-tuned [H], and it has no spreading function or tonality term, so it under-reads upward masking. Read with Aichinger's finding: a stem that is over about 90% masked is, in practice, not in the mix.
3. **Occupancy.** Per region, the mean number of stems within 6 dB of the loudest in a cell, weighted by energy: about 1 means one sound at a time, 2-3 a blend, more a wash.
4. **Per-region crest** of the mix (peak over RMS of the 10 ms band envelope): transient definition per band; a low-mid crest that collapses when layers are added is the measurable side of "mud".
Results on five tracks are in the table in section 1. The first lesson was the opposite of the fear: nothing in Spectre's drops is muddy, they are **under-occupied**. The analysis can therefore serve both directions: flag a crowd, and flag an empty role.

What a real version should add [H]: (a) the genre's EQ map as data (per role: high-pass point, cut region, owned region) and a check of each stem against it (energy below its high-pass point, energy in its cut region relative to its owned region), which needs each track to declare a `role`; (b) a proper masking threshold (the MPEG model's spreading function, as in Ronan et al.) once the simple version has been compared with listening verdicts; (c) the stack view: for the layers of one stack, the share each contributes per region, so a layer that adds nothing is visible; (d) a mono fold-down of the same numbers; (e) the low-mid build-up of the *mix* against the genre target (the 97-291 Hz bands of the era table).

What it cannot do: say that a mix is *good*. It can say that the pad cannot be heard, that the bass and the kick are in each other's way, that nobody is supplying air. Whether the result has depth is still a listening judgement (Hafezi and Reiss's caution).

Pestana's curves have two properties that matter here. They pool six decades of number-one singles, and the paper itself shows spectra getting flatter, with more and deeper low end, over those decades. And they average *magnitude* spectra, which under-reads sounds that are loud briefly and quiet in between (kicks), while the engine's analyser and the catalogue data below average power.

**What the published research says about change over time** [S]:
- Spectra of hits became flatter and more extended at both ends from the 1950s to 2010, with a marked rise in low end after about 2000 (Pestana et al. 2013).
- Bass levels in popular recordings rose from 1955 to 2016 (Hove, Vuust and Stupacher, JASA 2019, as cited by Gerdes and Siedenburg 2023 and by Deruty et al.; the paper itself could not be opened from this sandbox). Deruty, Meredith and Lattner's own analysis of 30,435 tracks, 1961-2022, shows "a clear increase in power in the lowest two bands over time".
- Loudness: Billboard year-end top 30 averaged -12.5 LUFS in 1990, -8.0 in 2010 and -8.8 in 2020, with PLR falling from 12.6 to 9.3 dB (Țînț and Drăgulin 2025, docs/MASTERING.md); mean loudness in the Million Song Dataset rose 1.2 dB per decade from the 1950s to the 2010s (Haghbayan, Coomes and Curran 2019).
- Lead vocals sat about 5 dB above the accompaniment in 1946 and fell to about 1-2 dB by 1975, where they stayed; by genre, 1990-2020: country +3.9 dB, rap +3.2, pop +2.7, rock +0.2, metal -3.1 (Gerdes and Siedenburg 2023). A measured per-genre target for the level of a voice or lead.
- Noisiness and inharmonicity of popular music rose until the mid-1980s and fell afterwards to 2022 (Deruty et al. 2023).
- No published numeric per-band spectrum exists for trance, or for any single genre after 2010: iZotope's targets ("Modern", "Bass Heavy", "Orchestral", and a 2024 hits curve) are plugin presets built from "numerous tracks" without published numbers; its Tonal Balance Control can build a custom target from a folder of the user's own files, which is the same idea as the proposal below.

Reading it:
1. **Every Pestana-derived curve shares one shape error against every genre measured:** too little in the kick-and-bass band (by 2.5-6 dB in the bass-driven genres) and 3-5 dB too much just above it, at 97-183 Hz. Its low-end hump sits a third of an octave to an octave too high. That it shows in all genres points at the method (magnitude averaging of sixty years of singles) more than at any one genre. In third-octave terms the interim fits put the peak at 63 Hz (trance, hip hop) or 100 Hz (pop rock) where the curves have it at 100-125 Hz.
2. **The top end error depends on the genre:** 2-6 dB too dark for trance, hip hop, pop rock and Latin; 8-11 dB too *bright* for orchestral scores and choirs, which borrow `rock`.
3. **Era matters less than genre and method.** Within one genre the periods differ by at most about 3.5 dB per band; the curves miss by more than that. The producer's question, "representative of the genre but not of today?", has the answer: representative of neither, mainly because of where the curves came from.
4. **Spectre's masters follow their curves,** as they should, and inherit the errors; where a mix lacks top-octave content the master ends up darker still, because the master may boost the top by at most 2 dB.
5. **Caveats, all of them:** tags are broad (`latin` includes reggaetón, `christmas` includes cathedral choirs, `folk` is mostly not Romanian, `soundtrack` is mostly large orchestra); the data are user-submitted and end in 2021 (thin after 2017); the lowest band is unreliable; nothing here measures 2023-2026.

**Getting era-appropriate targets: three sources, in order of quality.**
1. *Reference masters measured locally* (recommended as the standard). For each active genre and period, 20-30 commercial tracks chosen with the owner (and, for trance, with the producer who gave the feedback), bought as lossless downloads, analysed by a new `tools/refcurve.py` with the engine's own analyser: gated 1/3-octave spectrum (mid and side), integrated and short-term loudness, true peak, PLR, LRA, side-minus-mid width, low-end L/R correlation, crest per band. The repo receives the medians and spreads per band, the list of tracks measured (artist, title, year, label: facts), and the script. The audio lives in `~/.cache/spectre/refs/<genre>/`, outside git, as the chamber-instrument recordings and the singing corpus already do (docs/MANUAL.md; `tools/humanref.py`).
2. *The open catalogue* (used above): free, legal without any purchase, reproducible, and good enough to show a curve is wrong by 5 dB; too coarse (Bark bands, mono, no data after 2022, broad tags) to be the final target. The reference-album variant (the albums each genre doc already lists, measured the same way) is in the genre docs (joc, caroling, Latin pop, chamber score): it changes the picture for those genres, where tags are too broad. Against their own reference albums, 012 is about 10 dB heavy at 97-183 Hz and 5-10 dB bright at 3.7-7.7 kHz, 015 is 3-6.5 dB bright from 1.7 to 7.7 kHz, 010 is 3-5 dB heavy at 97-291 Hz, and 014's dark top turns out to be normal for boleros and acoustic records. The borrowed `rock` curve is 7-12 dB too bright for choirs and scores above 4 kHz.
3. *Published numbers.* For per-genre spectra after 2010 there are none (above). Published loudness, PLR and width figures stay in use (docs/MASTERING.md).

**The legal and ethical position, plainly** (not legal advice; the owner is in Denmark, an EU member state):
- *What is done:* a computer reads audio files the owner has lawfully bought and computes statistics (band energies, loudness). No audio, and nothing from which audio could be reconstructed, is stored in the repo, published, sampled, or used to train or prompt a generative model.
- *EU law:* Directive (EU) 2019/790, Article 4, obliges member states to allow "reproductions and extractions of lawfully accessible works" for text and data mining (the automated analysis of content to generate information such as patterns and trends); the copies may be kept as long as the mining needs them; the exception applies unless the rightholder has expressly reserved that use "in an appropriate manner, such as machine-readable means". The Danish implementation was not checked for this brief. Loading one's own purchased file into an analyser is in any case what every DJ program, loudness meter and reference-matching plugin does; iZotope ships a function that builds a target curve from a folder of the user's files.
- *Lines not to cross:* no ripping from streaming services or video sites (their terms forbid extraction, and it would not be "lawful access" in the sense above); no circumventing copy protection; no downloading from unlicensed sources; no committing, sharing or serving the files, even privately between agents; no excerpts on the website; no melodic, harmonic or arrangement material taken from a reference (standing rule 13); no claim that a Spectre track "matches" a named artist.
- *Publishing the results:* aggregate curves over 20 or more tracks and the list of titles measured are facts and may be published. A per-track spectrum is also a fact, but publish only aggregates: it is all the engine needs and it keeps the project clear of any appearance of copying one record's sound.
- *Ethics beyond law:* measuring what a genre's records have in common is how every mastering engineer learns a genre; reproducing one track's signature is not the aim, and the median over many tracks protects against it. The experiment's premise (music made by decisions, not by imitation of audio) is unaffected: the target is a ruler, not a source.
- **Recommendation:** adopt source 1 as the rule for every genre (and as item 4 of the new-genre checklist below); use the catalogue fits as interim targets only where they move a curve in a direction all the evidence agrees on (trance, hip hop, pop rock low end; scores and choirs top end); record each curve's provenance (tracks, years, method, date) next to it in `engine/genres.json`.

## 4. Correct versus compelling
**What the research says** [S].
- *Expectation is the mechanism.* Meyer (1956) argued that musical emotion arises when an expectation the music has set up is delayed or violated; Huron's ITPRA theory (2006) splits the response in time (imagination and tension before an event; prediction, reaction and appraisal after it) and explains the pleasure of a surprise by contrast: a fast, negative first reaction to the violated expectation, followed by a slower appraisal that it was harmless or beautiful (as summarised in Bannister and Eerola 2018; the books themselves were not re-read for this brief).
- *Surprise needs certainty around it.* Cheung and colleagues (Current Biology 2019) modelled about 80,000 chords in US Billboard pop songs: chords were rated most pleasant when they deviated strongly from expectation in a context of low uncertainty, or when they met expectation in a context of high uncertainty. Not surprise everywhere: one unexpected thing where the listener felt sure, or a sure thing where the listener was lost.
- *Chills follow the same shape.* In a 2024 study of 1,806 chill onsets in 1,019 pieces (IDyOM melodic model), melodic uncertainty fell and surprise rose around the onset of chills: "surprising events" in a context of relatively low surprisal. The same study is the honest measure of how far this goes: a classifier built on those features predicted chills with an AUC of 0.60 (0.5 is chance).
- *What the moments are made of.* Shivers were linked to new or unprepared harmony and to sudden changes of texture and dynamics (Sloboda 1991); to crescendos (Panksepp 1995); to rises in loudness and sharpness and the entry of a new voice or instrument (Grewe et al. 2007); to an expansion of the frequency range and to a solo instrument emerging from its accompaniment (Guhn et al. 2007): all as summarised by Bannister and Eerola (2018), whose own experiment found that cutting the "chills section" out of a piece reduced chills only marginally (N = 24). Some listeners never get chills at all.
- *In dance music the release is the drop, and it is mostly a kept promise.* Solberg and Dibben (2019; 24 listeners, skin conductance): the break routine "sets up, delays, and gratifies expectations"; the strongest response went to the track with a contrasting breakdown, long uplifters with a volume increase and a drum roll ending in a syncopated figure; skin conductance followed amplitude and spectral flux, not RMS loudness. In trance specifically, fans' enjoyment of chord progressions rose with complexity up to a point and then fell (TRANCE.md "Harmony").
- *Groove and imperfection.* The belief that small timing imperfections make the groove is not what experiments find. Senn and colleagues (2016; 159 listeners, funk and swing by professional players): fully quantized and original timing were rated equally high; exaggerated deviations were rated worse, experts noticing sooner; the fitted optimum lay between quantized and original. Frühauf and Davies and their colleagues (2013) found quantized versions rated highest; Butterfield (2010) found listeners often could not detect deviations up to 30 ms; Madison found no relation between the size of microtiming and groove, but relations with event density, beat salience and syncopation (all as reviewed by Senn et al.). When timing is loosened by a machine, its *structure* matters: listeners preferred humanizing with long-range-correlated deviations, the kind human players produce, over independent random jitter (Hennig et al. 2011).
- *Low end is part of groove.* In an experiment with dance-music loops, filtering the low frequencies out of the bass line lowered both groove and liking ratings clearly; the measure that tracked the ratings was activity in the 50-100 Hz band (Lustig and Tan 2019). That is the band where Spectre's trance masters are 6-7 dB short (TRANCE.md, C).
- *Character from the sound itself.* "When a track is too tight, it can sound a little sterile and lifeless": offset the delay times, adjust pre-delays, don't over-tighten (Ferry Corsten, MusicRadar 2017). "Do the unexpected"; a big surprise in the middle of repeated material hits hardest (MusicRadar 2007). Free-running oscillators give every note a different start (Szabo; Perfect Circuit).

**What follows for a producer agent** [H]: the difference between correct and compelling is, as far as research can say, a difference of *planned expectation*, and it can be worked on deliberately.
1. **Build certainty first, then spend one surprise.** Per track, name in the journal the expectation each section sets (the hook's contour, the chord loop, the fill every 8 bars, the place the drop lands) and the one or two places where it will be broken: a note, a chord, a silence, an entry, a missing beat. Sessions 007 (one changed note), 013 (the roll that stops early) and 014 (the crossed-out phrase) did this, and the session notes name those moments as the ones the tracks turn on.
2. **Prefer the documented triggers** when choosing the surprise: unprepared harmony; a sudden change of texture or dynamics in either direction; the entry of a new voice; a widening of the frequency range (the air layer or the sub arriving); a solo line stepping out of its accompaniment.
3. **Don't confuse surprise with randomness.** Random variation everywhere raises uncertainty and lowers the effect of the one event that matters (Cheung). Variation belongs in the supporting layers and the humanizing; the surprise is one decision.
4. **Imperfection by structure, not by jitter.** In machine genres (trance) keep the grid and put the life in the sound: free-running phases, slow drift of detune, filter and pan, round-robin variants, delays offset from the grid, a layer that enters slightly late on purpose. In played genres use consistent placement (a snare that always sits 20 ms late, as in 008), correlated drift across a phrase (Hennig), syncopation and ghost notes, and stay inside what Senn found tolerable: at or below the looseness of a good player, never exaggerated. The engine's `humanize_ms` is independent jitter, the kind listeners liked least.
5. **One ugly thing, taught early.** A deliberate flaw (a dead stroke, a bent note, a clipped word, a filter that opens too far once) reads as character when it returns and means something (014); alone it reads as an error.
6. **Check the plan against the render**: after rendering, verify that the surprise is actually audible (its stem's own share in that bar; the section's loudness or spectral-flux step), because a surprise buried in the mix is only a plan.

**What an AI can and cannot judge, plainly.**
- *Can:* whether a render matches measurable intentions (levels, bands, timing, masking, loudness steps, the genre target, word intelligibility); whether the planned surprise exists in the audio; whether an expectation was set up often enough to be broken (count the repetitions); how predictable a melody is against its own earlier statements (a small n-gram model in the style of IDyOM is feasible).
- *Cannot:* hear. Spectre reads spectrograms and numbers. It has no access to whether a timbre is beautiful, dated or cheap, whether a groove feels good, or whether a moment moves anyone; the best published predictor of chills is barely above chance even with the audio. Its sense of "compelling" is second-hand, from descriptions of music, and it is biased toward what is describable and countable. The project's own record says the same: the ears missed the clap that ruined 007, the static on phones, and the sound-design gap a trance producer heard in seconds.
- *Therefore the owner's ear, and now a professional's, are part of the instrument, not a review at the end.* Proposed protocol: (a) **sound audition before arrangement**: for the two or three sounds a track stands on (kick, bass, lead; snare, guitar; the voice), render 2-3 candidate stacks as 8-bar loops and let the owner choose by ear before the session builds on them: cheap, and it is where 007 went wrong twice; (b) **one mid-session checkpoint** at the first full drop or chorus; (c) verdicts recorded as symptoms with a timestamp ("0:55 thin", "the pad disappears"), never as prescriptions (standing rule 10 already does this for voices); (d) every verdict logged next to the measurements of that version in docs/LESSONS.md, so that over time the numbers that agree with human ears can be told from those that don't. That log is the only way the "ears" can be calibrated; no amount of analysis replaces it.
- *An audio-capable critic model* (AGENTS.md, known limit 1) could add a weak second opinion on timbre; it is unvalidated here and must not replace (a)-(d).

## 5. Checklist: what the research for a NEW genre must produce
Standing rules 7 and 8 (docs/ORCHESTRATION.md) already ask for sourced genre settings and industry mastering practice; standing rule 16 (2026-11-25) adds layering, carving and a current target. The genre brief `docs/<GENRE>.md` is complete only when it also has:
1. **Layer roles.** For every part of the genre's ensemble (low end, rhythm, harmony, lead, voice, percussion, space): which sources make it up, what each supplies (sub, body, mid bite, air, transient, width, centre), how many, at which octaves, and which role each source must *not* play. From producer or engineer sources for that genre; where none exist, say so and derive it from the genre's instrumentation, marked [H].
2. **An EQ map.** A table per instrument: high-pass point, region to cut or control, region it owns, with a source per row and a "cross-check" against at least one independent source; disagreements noted, not averaged away. For acoustic and orchestral genres the map states how light the moves are and that orchestration and balance come first.
3. **Glue.** How the genre makes layers one instrument and the band one record: buses, bus compression and saturation, shared spaces, sidechain habits, with typical amounts.
4. **An era-appropriate master target with provenance.** Which period the genre brief aims at (normally the last five years, or the classic period the brief asks for); a per-band target from measurements of that period (reference masters measured locally, or the open catalogue data as an interim), with the number of tracks, the years, the method and the tag or list used; loudness, PLR, width and low-end mono point from the same set. A borrowed or pooled curve may be used only as a stop-gap and must be labelled as one in `engine/genres.json`.
5. **The genre's low-end and top-end facts:** the kick or bass fundamental range, how far the sub extends, what supplies the top octave (cymbals, air layers, bows, breath), so the mix is built toward the target instead of being equalised onto it.
6. **What is characteristic imperfection in this genre** and what is simply wrong: timing feel (grid-tight, laid back, rubato), tuning practice, noise that belongs (bow, breath, fret) and noise that doesn't.
7. **The nearest wrong *sounds*** next to the nearest wrong feelings: which sound-design choices would make it read as another decade or another genre.
8. **A census of a reference arrangement** where a published multitrack breakdown exists: how many sources sound at once per region at the peak section.
9. **An entry in the remix table** of this document if earlier tracks in a neighbouring genre are affected.

## 6. Gaps and proposals
Ranked by expected effect on what a listener hears, per unit of work [H]. Nothing here has been built.

### Engine
1. **True targets for the master** (largest effect, least work).
   - `tools/refcurve.py`: measure a folder of reference masters with `master.py`'s analyser and write a curve plus its provenance; a `tools/catalogue_ltas.py` that reproduces this brief's open-data tables and fits (the scripts for this brief were scratch and are not in the repo: method in TRANCE.md, C).
   - New curves in `engine/master.py`, never replacing old ones (forward only): interim `trance_catalogue`, `hiphop_catalogue`, `rock_catalogue` (the fitted values in the genre docs), later `<genre>_<year>` curves from reference masters; own curves for `chamberscore` and `caroling`, which today borrow a curve 8-11 dB too bright for them.
   - `engine/genres.json`: per genre `target_source`, `target_period`, `target_tracks`, `target_date`; a `borrowed: true` flag that makes the master print a warning.
   - Report additions: deviation per region from the target *before* EQ (so the producer mixes toward it), and the low-mid band (about 100-300 Hz) as a named check.
   - Keep: the +2 dB cap on top-end boosts. A brighter target must be met by the sounds.
2. **Ears for stems: `listen --clarity`** (section 2): layer census, masked and own share per stem, occupancy, per-region crest; tracks declare a `role`; the genre's EQ map stored as data (`engine/eqmaps.json` or inside genres.json) and each stem checked against it; warnings "role empty in a peak section", "stem over 90% masked", "more than four sources within 10 dB at 150-400 Hz" (thresholds [H], to be tuned against listening verdicts). AGENTS.md calls the ears the bottleneck; this is the cheapest widening of it (prototype: 14 s per section).
3. **Layer stacks and buses.**
   - `stack`: one pattern played by several layers, each with its instrument, octave or semitone offset, level, pan and width, a start offset in ms, its own envelope overrides and a `role` that pulls a default EQ from the genre's map (high-pass, cut, emphasis as a preset, adjustable).
   - `bus`: named groups (a stack is one automatically) with their own fx chain, sends and sidechain; bus compression, saturation and the new `ott` there. Section automation addressing a bus.
   - Return processing: EQ on the reverb and delay returns, ducking of a return by its source or by the kick, more than one reverb (a short room and a long hall), pre-delay per reverb.
4. **A current synthesizer voice** (for trance first, useful in pop and hip hop):
   - `supersaw` v2 (opt-in, so 007 and 013 still reproduce): the pitch-tracking high-pass, the mix curve that lowers the centre, a nonlinear `detune` control, optional second supersaw an octave apart, a sub oscillator, a noise/air oscillator, per-voice filter spread.
   - Movement: two LFOs and a slow random drift, assignable to detune, cutoff, pan, level and pulse width; delayed vibrato; a second envelope; key tracking of the filter; velocity to filter and attack.
   - A general `polysynth` with oscillator choices (saw, square/pulse, sine, triangle, noise, a small wavetable set) and unison on any of them.
   - New fx: `ott` (three bands split near 90 Hz and 2.5 kHz, upward and downward compression, amount and time: MusicRadar's description), `dyneq` (a band cut keyed by another track: the 808-under-kick notch), `clip` (soft clipper for kick and drum bus: docs/proposals/mastering-trance-poprock.md already asks for it), `mseq` (mid/side EQ), a ping-pong delay with offsets from the grid.
5. **Low end and top end sources.** `clubkick` as separable layers (tuned sub sine, punch, click) or a `kickstack` preset; a `sub` instrument (sine with a short attack and release, mono, low-passed) for trance and hip hop; hats and cymbals with tonal metal partials for the electronic kits, so a brighter target can be met without the noise that reads as static; an air layer source (filtered saw-noise hybrid).
6. **Determinism per track.** Instruments draw from one shared random stream, so adding or reordering a track changes the random phases of the others (seen in this research: solo renders don't null against the mix). Seed each track's stream from the project seed and the track name. Without it a remix cannot leave untouched tracks sample-identical.
7. **Structured humanizing.** `humanize` with long-range-correlated drift (Hennig et al.) and per-phrase push and pull, next to today's independent jitter; round-robin variants for synth notes (phase, detune, filter) as the electronic counterpart.
8. **Expectation tools** (small): a report of how predictable each melody note is given the track's own earlier statements (an n-gram model in the style of IDyOM), and of section-to-section steps in loudness, spectral flux and frequency range, so the planned surprise can be checked in the render.

### Producer prompt and process
1. Read PRODUCTION.md and the genre's "Layering and EQ carving" before stage 1; in the first journal entry list the track's parts and, for each, its layers and roles.
2. **Sound stage before arrangement:** build the two or three sounds the track stands on as stacks; render each as an 8-bar loop in two or three variants; where the owner is available, he picks by ear (section 4); journal the choice.
3. Give every track a `role`; run the clarity analysis on each peak section before the first trial master and after every added layer; a layer that fills no empty region comes out again.
4. Mix toward the genre's target regions by building them (a sub, an air layer), not by EQ at the end; if the master reports its EQ at the limit against a curve marked borrowed or under review, report it and do not carve the sound to fit.
5. Keep an **expectation ledger** in the journal: what each section teaches the listener to expect, and the one or two places where the track breaks it; verify after rendering that the break is audible.
6. Imperfection by structure (section 4, point 4), named in the journal; no added randomness for its own sake.
7. Check every stack in mono and on the phone view.
8. Orchestrator: for every new genre, the research checklist in section 5; log every listening verdict next to the measurements of that version (docs/LESSONS.md).
