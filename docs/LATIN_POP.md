# Latin pop (intimate strands): a working brief

For sessions that make an instrumental in the world of Latin pop when the subject is close and personal: bachata, the bolero and romantic ballad, the Latin pop ballad, Latin acoustic pop and the soft, romantic side of reggaetón-pop. A vocabulary, not a rulebook. Researched 2026-11-24 for session 014 ("Love letter"). Spectre makes instrumentals: a voice is an instrument here (a refrain fragment, a call, a line the requinto answers; see "Voices"), never a sung song carried by a lead vocal.

### The mastering decision: `latinpop`, the `rock` curve reused, -11 LUFS
- **Loudness -11 LUFS.** The intimate strands sit at the quiet end of the measured range: boleros about -13, acoustic pop -11, ballads -10.2, modern bachata -9.7, reggaeton-pop -10.6; Sage Audio advises about -12 for a dynamic Latin master. -11 is a choice inside that range (between the bolero and the modern bachata medians), not a measured median of one strand. A dembow-driven reggaeton-pop track could go 1-2 dB louder (`--lufs -10`), a bare bolero 1-2 dB quieter (`--lufs -12.5`): only with a reason, noted in the journal.
- **Curve: `rock`, reused**: it is the mean of Pestana et al.'s *pop* and *rock* average spectra, the closest measured curve to a Latin pop record (no Latin curve exists). It asks for 100-400 Hz body (bass guitar, guitar bodies, piano) and a natural top-end decay that suits nylon guitars and voice. `eq` 0.6 (hand-tuned): lighter than the band genres because the curve is borrowed; `translate` 0.3: most strands have a bass guitar or bass line.
- **Test** (`examples/latinpop-test.json`: 8 bars of bachata derecho and majao on the existing instruments (requinto on `acguitar` an octave up with a chorus, segunda, bass, bongó on two high `tom`s, güira on `shaker`) and a Spanish `hanami-es-soft` line; 128 bpm): `master --genre latinpop` reaches -11.0 LUFS, -1.07 dBTP, PLR 10.0, no warnings, top end within 3.5 dB of the curve (5 kHz -3.5). The EQ sits at its limits at 100-200 Hz (-13 dB at 125 Hz after EQ) and 31-40 Hz: a nylon-guitar-and-bass band has a hole where the curve wants body, the same as `examples/poprock-test.json` against this curve. The first test, with the bass down to E1/F1, also put 40 Hz 7.8 dB over the curve: keep bass lines on A1 and above. Give the bass body at 100-150 Hz and the guitars at 180-250 Hz in the mix, and accept some of the hole: a bare bolero or bachata is lighter at 125 Hz than a pop-rock record. **Status: reused measured curve, hand-tuned EQ strength and loudness choice; replace with a curve measured on Latin references when one exists.** *(2026-11-25: superseded in part. The default is now `latinpop_ref`; a dembow-driven track measures 3.5-7 dB heavier at 31.5-63 Hz than it, so it belongs with the urbano target, and each strand has its own loudness: "Granularity", section D.)*

## What Latin pop covers today, and which strand fits an intimate subject
"A subgenre of popular music defined primarily as pop music performed in Spanish or Portuguese", built on international pop conventions with Latin American and Iberian traditions; it grew from the romantic ballads of the 1970s (Julio Iglesias, Roberto Carlos), and "this blend of Latinized dance-pop and adult contemporary balladeering dominated Latin pop through the 1990s" ([Wikipedia, Latin pop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_pop)). Since the 2000s reggaetón has merged into it ("La Tortura", "Hips Don't Lie", and "Despacito", which opened a "new Latin crossover era": [Wikipedia, Reggaeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reggaeton)); urban artists also release piano-driven or acoustic love ballads, softer than the perreo sound, some with a "mild hint of urban beats" ([Billboard, Latin urban artists singing love ballads](https://www.billboard.com/index.php/articles/columns/latin/8514655/latin-urban-artists-singing-love-ballads)). The strands, and when each suits a close, personal feeling:

| strand | what it is | carries | suits | risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bachata** | Dominican guitar music (first recording 1962, "Borracho de amor"); first called *amargue* ("bitterness"), stigmatized under Trujillo; electric guitar from 1987; *Bachata Rosa* (Juan Luis Guerra) brought it worldwide; Aventura and Romeo Santos made it urban; Prince Royce fused it with R&B and pop. Earlier lyrics were "mostly about cheating relationships and hurt feelings", "often bittersweet"; modern lyrics are "more often about romantic love" ([Wikipedia, Bachata (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachata_(music))) | the requinto's arpeggios over a steady 8th-note pulse; ache with a body that sways | longing, devotion, a bittersweet ache that still moves; the letter as something you could dance to slowly | the club bachata (fast, loud, the mambo as a party); the bitter *amargue* if the letter is not bitter |
| **Bolero / romantic ballad** | born in Santiago de Cuba (Pepe Sánchez, "Tristezas", 1883), "the quintessential Latin American romantic song of the twentieth century", "sophisticated lyrics dealing with love"; guitar trios (Trío Los Panchos), the bolero-son (Trío Matamoros), Mexico's trova yucateca, the Havana *filin* ("feeling"), Luis Miguel's revival with *Romance* ([Wikipedia, Bolero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolero)); filin was "a Cuban, but US-influenced" song fashion, crooning after the microphone, with roots in the bolero ([Wikipedia, Filin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filin_(music))) | a slow, gentle sway; harmony that leans and resolves; one guitar and one voice close to the microphone | tenderness, intimacy, a confession; the letter read slowly, every word weighed | melodrama (big strings, a crooner's sob), the wedding-band standard, nostalgia for an era instead of a person |
| **Latin pop ballad** | piano, strings, a build to a big last chorus (Alejandro Sanz, Sin Bandera, Reik, Camila, Jesse & Joy) | a melody that grows; the arrangement as a slow swell | declaration, the moment it is finally said | the generic radio love song; the power-ballad climax as a habit |
| **Latin acoustic pop** | guitars, small percussion, light rhythm (Morat, Natalia Lafourcade's *Hasta la raíz*); folk colours (cuatro, charango) and a living-room sound | specificity: small sounds, close and dry | the personal and specific, a letter in someone's handwriting | a café-playlist mood with nothing at stake |
| **Soft reggaetón-pop ("romantic dembow")** | dembow at about 90-100 bpm under acoustic guitar or piano, sung sweetly (Camilo, Sebastián Yatra, Reik's *Ahora*, Karol G's "Ocean"); bachatón joined reggaetón to bachata's guitar ([Wikipedia, Reggaeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reggaeton)) | a body groove under a tender line | warmth with a pulse; a modern, playful intimacy | the club banger (a full dembow kit, 808 pressure, perreo) |

**Recommended for an intimate love letter:** a **bolero-bachata** reading: the bachata's requinto and 8th-note pulse (derecho) at its slower end, or the bolero's sway at 72-88, with the bolero's harmony and closeness; the majao and the mambo used sparingly as the letter's turns, not as a party. A hint of soft dembow can carry a modern verse if it stays under the guitar. The producer chooses; this is a recommendation, not a rule.

## Instruments and roles
- **Requinto (lead guitar):** "the lead melodic instrument in a bachata band"; it "riffs off the vocal melody"; a common verse riff is an "ascending or descending arpeggiated chord", one note on each 8th, eight notes a bar (Ubisoft part 3). The requinto is "a smaller version of a classical guitar, with a scale length between 52-54cm", "tuned a fourth higher than a standard classical guitar, to A2-D3-G3-C4-E4-A4", created by Alfredo Bojalil Gil, "the third voice and requintist" of Los Panchos, and used in Mexico's *trío romántico* groups ([Wikipedia, Requinto guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Requinto_guitar)). Traditional bachata used "a nylon-string Spanish guitar and maracas"; modern bachata from the 1990s an "electric steel string" guitar and the güira (Wikipedia, Bachata (music)); the modern bachata requinto is commonly heard with a chorus effect (craft observation, unsourced).
- **Segunda (rhythm guitar):** plays all eight 8ths, "with low 'bass' notes on downbeats one, three, and four"; at its entrance it "strums every upbeat and the second downbeat", "a syncopated feel against the percussion" (Ubisoft part 3).
- **Bass guitar:** mirrors the segunda's bass notes on beats 1, 3 and 4 but "drops the second downbeat, keeping only the pickup into the third downbeat": "1, (2) and 3, 4" (Ubisoft part 3); in the mambo, slaps and swoops. In the bolero, "half note followed by two quarter notes" ([PBS, a bolero](https://www.pbs.org/buenavista/music/a_bolero.html)).
- **Bongó:** two drums, "the larger hembra" and "the smaller macho"; in Cuban music the bongosero "often plays a continuous eight-stroke pattern called martillo", an ostinato of 8ths; "the bongo became integral to bachata" ([Wikipedia, Bongo drum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bongo_drum)). In bachata's derecho the bongó plays all eight 8ths, accenting 1, 3 and 4, highest on 1 and 3 and lowest on 4, sometimes a roll on 4; in the majao "just the downbeats", equally; in the mambo, improvised fills (Ubisoft part 2).
- **Güira:** a metal scraper played with a brush; derecho: all eight 8ths "with equal emphasis"; majao: "all four downbeats here with slightly longer scrapes"; mambo: a strict "1, 2-and 3, 4-and 1" (Ubisoft part 2).
- **Maracas, claves, congas, timbales:** in the bolero "Bongos, maracas, claves, congas, or timbales may appear depending on the arrangement, region, and era"; "guitar or piano often carries the harmony with arpeggios, broken chords, or soft syncopated hits"; "Bass supports the harmonic movement and usually avoids overcrowding the vocal line" (Soundbrenner).
- **Piano and strings:** the Latin pop ballad and the orchestral bolero (Luis Miguel's *Romance* era); filin brought jazz-age crooning and close harmony (Wikipedia, Filin).
- **Dembow kit (reggaetón-pop):** kick, snare, clap, hats, shaker, timbal rolls, with "kick, kickdown, palito, snare, timbal, timballroll, and sometimes hi-hat" as the percussion elements (Wikipedia, Reggaeton); 808 or synth bass.

## Rhythm patterns per instrument (engine notation, 16 steps = one 4/4 bar)
Every pattern below is written from the sources above; velocities and the exact strokes inside them are Spectre's reading (craft). One track per drum stroke type (as with the darbuka).

**Bachata, derecho (verses; 120-135 bpm):**
```
"bongo_high": "X.x.X.x.X.x.x.x."      // all 8ths, accents on 1 and 3 (highest pitch)
"bongo_low":  "............X..."      // the lowest hit on beat 4 (sometimes a roll: "............R...")
"guira":      "x.x.x.x.x.x.x.x."      // all 8ths, equal; give it dyn [1, .6, .9, .6] only if it sounds mechanical
"segunda_bass": "x.......x...x..."    // low notes on 1, 3, 4 (voicing root, register A2)
"segunda":    "..x.x.x...x...x."      // strums on every upbeat and on beat 2 (chop 0.8-0.9)
"bass":       "0:A1:5 6:A1:2 8:D2:4 12:E2:4"   // 1, (2)&, 3, 4: the pickup into 3
"requinto":   {"style": "arp", "rate": 2}      // one note per 8th, up or down the chord
```
**Bachata, majao (choruses):** `bongo_high` `"x...x...x......."`, `bongo_low` `"............x..."` (four downbeats equal, 4 lowest); `guira` `"X...X...X...X..."` (downbeats, longer scrapes: a longer decay). Guitars and bass as in the derecho.
**Bachata, mambo (bridge or interlude):** `guira` `"x.....x.x.....x."` (1, 2&, 3, 4&); bongó fills, the bass slaps and swoops, the requinto solos; the singer stops.
**Bolero (72-90 bpm, 4/4):** bass `"0:A1:8 8:E2:4 12:A2:4"` (half, quarter, quarter); low pulse on 1 and 3 with soft chords on the and of 2 and the and of 4: `"x.......x......."` plus chords `"......x.......x."` (Soundbrenner's basic pattern); bongó martillo `"x.x.x.x.x.x.x.x."` (eight strokes, the accents Spectre's choice); maracas in 8ths; guitar or piano arpeggios. The **cinquillo** (2/4: "eighth, sixteenth, eighth, sixteenth, eighth"): `"x.xx.xx."` per half bar.
**Son clave (3-2), for bolero-son colours:** `"x..x..x...x.x..."` ("strokes on 1, 1a, 2&, 3&, 4"; the first three strokes are the tresillo: [Wikipedia, Clave (rhythm)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clave_(rhythm))).
**Dembow (soft reggaetón-pop, 90-100 bpm):** kick `"x...x...x...x..."` (four on the floor), snare `"...x..x....x..x."` (steps 4, 7, 12, 15 counted from 1; an optional extra hit on step 10: `"...x..x..x.x..x."`), clap doubling the first snare, closed hat on quarters, shaker in 16ths with "two quiet, two loud" velocity pairs and about 50% swing (MusicRadar); "the 3+3+2 (tresillo) rhythm, complemented by a bass drum in 4/4 time" (Wikipedia, Reggaeton; [Dembow beat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dembow_beat): "boom-ch-boom-chick", from Steely & Clevie's "Fish Market" / "Poco Man Jam" riddim, named after Shabba Ranks' "Dem Bow"). For a romantic verse keep it light: rim or a soft snare, no 808 pressure, the guitar on top.

## Harmony
- **Major keys dominate** these strands by measurement (see Keys above); minor is a choice for a bitter or haunted letter, not the default.
- **Bachata and bolero vocabulary** (craft, from the repertoire; no musicological source was found in this pass): diatonic I-IV-V with the V always a dominant seventh, secondary dominants into IV and V, the minor iv borrowed in a major key for the ache, a diminished passing chord between I and ii, minor verses that open into the relative or parallel major in the chorus; the bolero and filin add the crooner's sevenths and ninths (maj7, m7, 9, dominant 7b9 leading home). The requinto arpeggiates these chords and fills phrase ends with runs in thirds or sixths.
- **Latin pop ballad:** pop progressions (I-V-vi-IV and its rotations), a key lift before the last chorus as a habit to question.
- **Reggaetón-pop:** two- to four-chord loops, often minor in reggaetón at large (73% minor, Mixgraph) but major in the romantic pop strand (catalogue).

- **What a voice may do here:** a refrain fragment (three to six words), a call that the requinto answers, a word held under the guitar, a wordless "ah" doubling the requinto in the mambo; never verses. Bachata and bolero are sung genres, so the voice can matter, but it stays an instrument.

## Mixing the voice (researched 2026-11-24)
The principles for every genre, the choir research and the check procedure are in [docs/voice/MIXING.md](voice/MIXING.md); this section is what changes for an intimate Latin voice. Values marked *(hand-tuned)* are Spectre's starting points. No engineer's account of a bachata or bolero vocal mix with settings was found (searched in English and Spanish); the sources below are a Latin pop hit, a ballad mix and a crooner session, plus measured vocal levels for pop.

**What the research says**
- **The voice comes first and everything is balanced around it.** Humberto Gatica (mixing a crooner with a big band, the nearest documented session to a bolero singer) starts by getting the vocal right and then sets rhythm, horns and strings around it, compresses it only "a little bit" and rides the dynamics by hand; the space is real chambers and a digital room, not a wash ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/inside-track-michael-buble-youre-nobody-till-somebody-loves-you)). Natalia Lafourcade's bolero set moves from "soft, almost whispered bolero songs" to full arrangements, with a band she wants "organic" ([Mix, 2018](https://www.mixonline.com/?p=3738)).
- **Measured level:** in chart pop the lead vocal sits about 2.7 dB over the accompaniment on average, and solo artists 3.5 dB higher than bands; country, the most text-led genre measured, 3.9 dB ([Gerdes & Siedenburg 2023](https://www.geidai.ac.jp/~marui/course_enshu_2024/gerdes_siedenburg_202304.pdf); A-weighted levels after source separation, 1990-2020). No Latin genre was measured; a sung bolero or bachata is a solo voice over a small band, the high end of that range.
- **A Latin pop lead chain with numbers** (the Despacito remix vocal bus, Josh Gudwin): a channel compressor at ratio 3 and threshold -12, EQ of about +2 dB at 4.5 kHz and -3 dB at 250 Hz, an optical leveller after it, a second compressor at about 2:1, a de-esser working at 7 and 14 kHz, a plate (EMT 140) on a send, quarter- and 8th-note delay throws on single words only, and a widened copy of the whole vocal 20 dB down ([Tingen](https://tingen.org/making-of-bieber-despacito-remix/)).
- **A ballad's space with numbers** (Mike Senior's mix of a ballad): a long soft hall of 3.5 s behind a 50 ms pre-delay with its lows rolled off from 200 Hz, a plate confined to 1-5 kHz behind 16 ms, an ambience under 100 ms to lock the voice into the band, a quarter-note delay on phrase ends, all sends de-essed, 4 dB of low-mid shelving cut from about 450 Hz, and detailed level automation for "lyric intelligibility" ([Sound On Sound, Mix Rescue](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/mix-rescue-jay-menon)). The long hall works there because it is quiet, late and filtered; the voice itself stays close.
- **Mastering:** bachata since 2010 sits about -9.7 LUFS and boleros about -13 (catalogue below). At `latinpop`'s -11 the limiter is light; the voice changes through the master's tonal EQ, which shares 2-8 kHz with the requinto and the güira and cannot move one without the others. Mid-range crowding is the genre's known mastering problem (Sage Audio, in Configuration). Fix a masked voice in the mix (requinto register, güira level), never by asking the master for presence.

**The engine, measured:** `mix.reverb` `size` 0.35 decays in 0.9 s, 0.45 in about 1.05 s, 0.5 in 1.15 s, 0.6 in 1.4 s, 0.75 in 2.1 s. One reverb bus, one delay bus, no de-esser.

**Starting chain: a sung fragment over requinto, guitar and bongó** (*hand-tuned*, built from the two documented chains above and the blend study in MANUAL "Voices"):
```json
"mix": {"reverb": {"size": 0.55, "damping": 0.6, "return_db": -7, "lpf": 6500},
        "delay":  {"beats": 1, "feedback": 0.25, "return_db": -10, "lpf": 3500}},
"voz": {"inst": {"type": "singer", "source": "svs", "voice": "hanami-es", "inhale": 0.3,
                 "svs_fade_in_ms": 30, "svs_fade_out_ms": 120},
        "send_reverb": 0.14, "reverb_predelay_ms": 50, "send_delay": 0.0,
        "fx": [{"type": "hpf", "hz": 100}, {"type": "eq", "hz": 250, "gain_db": -2, "q": 0.8},
               {"type": "eq", "hz": 800, "gain_db": -2, "q": 0.9}, {"type": "eq", "hz": 4500, "gain_db": 2, "q": 0.8},
               {"type": "comp", "threshold_db": -22, "ratio": 3}, {"type": "highshelf", "hz": 8000, "gain_db": 3},
               {"type": "gain", "db": 6}]}
```
- **Close, not dry:** `send_reverb` 0.10-0.18 behind a 40-60 ms pre-delay; bolero and ballad may take a longer, quieter space (`size` 0.75, send 0.10-0.14, pre-delay 60-80) and bachata a shorter one (`size` 0.45-0.55). The quarter-note delay only on the last word of a phrase: put that word on a second track with `send_delay` 0.4-0.6 and no dry level change, or leave the delay out.
- **Voice:** `hanami-es` for words over a bachata band (the requinto and güira are bright); `hanami-es-soft` for a bolero or a bare guitar, where its dark, airy tone (tilt about 11 dB darker than the root voice) is the intimacy. Under a full band the soft voice needs the 4.5 kHz bell at 3-4 dB and still loses consonants: prefer changing the arrangement under it.
- **Level:** 1-3 LU over the band under it *(hand-tuned from the pop figure; the fragment is the lead while it sounds)*; the requinto answers in the gaps instead of playing through the words, which is the genre's own call-and-response and the cheapest unmasking there is.
- **Doubles and width:** none on the lead fragment, or `double` 0.1-0.15 with `double_pan` 0.7 for a chorus word (the documented double sat 20 dB down). Centre. A wordless "ah" doubling the requinto is a texture: `hanami-es-soft`, `svs_vibrato` 0.5, send 0.25, 4-6 LU under.
- **Compression:** one stage at 3:1 is the documented centre; the engine's `comp` eats level, so end the chain with `gain`.
- **Spanish and the mix:** the five vowels are steady and carry well; b, d and g between vowels are soft by rule (languages/es.md) and are the first sounds a reverb or a guitar hides, and a word split across two patterns or entered through a filter loses its first consonant. Keep each sung word inside one phrase with a rest before it.

**How to check:** Whisper `small` (multilingual) forced to Spanish on the bars with words, on the mix and on the voice alone, as `examples/vocals/svs/spanish.py` does for dry lines (procedure in MIXING.md "Checking a voice"); the vocal-versus-band loudness from a render with `voz` muted. The dry voice reads 96%: if the mix reads much lower and the voice alone still reads, it is masking or space, not pronunciation.

## Nearest wrong feelings
- **A club reggaetón banger:** full dembow kit, 808 pressure, perreo energy, a drop. The letter has a pulse, not a dancefloor.
- **A cheesy wedding-band ballad:** the standard played to please a room: sugary strings, every cliché fill, a modulation for applause.
- **A tourist "Latin" cliché:** "Latin" as decoration: castanets, flamenco flourishes, a Spanish-guitar postcard, "olé"; flamenco is Spanish, not Caribbean or Mexican, and none of these strands use castanets.
- **Melodrama:** the sob in every phrase, a crescendo that shouts what a whisper would say better, telenovela strings.
- **A generic radio love song:** the four-chord ballad with nothing specific in it, a mood that could be about anyone.

## Layering and EQ carving (researched 2026-11-25)
The cross-genre method is in [PRODUCTION.md](PRODUCTION.md). Labels: **[M]** measured, **[S]** a named source, **[P]** carried over from a professional trance producer's EQ map (2026) where it applies, **[H]** hand-tuned. No mixing guide specific to bachata or bolero was found; guitar and bass ranges apply general guidance, the percussion notes come from Sound On Sound's Latin percussion article.

**Layer roles.** An intimate Latin band is a small acoustic stack with fixed jobs ("Instruments and roles" above):
| Part | Layers and their jobs | Label |
|---|---|---|
| **Lead (requinto, or the voice)** | the requinto alone, bright and forward; often with a chorus effect, which is a built-in double; answered, not doubled, by the voice | [S] this brief; the chorus remark is unsourced there |
| **Harmony (segunda)** | all eight 8ths with bass notes on 1, 3 and 4: the body layer under the requinto, an octave lower in feel; in the bolero, arpeggios or soft syncopated hits; piano or strings as a pad layer in the ballad | [S] this brief |
| **Bass** | one bass, sparse, "avoids overcrowding the vocal line" | [S] this brief |
| **Percussion** | one layer made of two bands: **bongó** in the mids (martillo) and **güira or maracas** on top; they share the 8ths, so they must not share a frequency range | [S] this brief; the band split [H] |
| **Voice** | lead with a soft double; a second voice in thirds (the trío romántico) | [S] this brief |

**EQ map** (starting ranges; gentle on the guitars, they are the sound of the genre):
| Instrument | High-pass | Cut / control | Emphasise | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Requinto (lead guitar) | 100-150 Hz [H] (its lowest string is A2, 110 Hz) | 200-400 Hz mud | 1.5-2.5 kHz presence, 2-5 kHz when it leads; 8-12 kHz air | iZotope EQ cheat sheet (acoustic guitar); [P] plucks |
| Segunda (rhythm guitar) | 70-100 Hz | 200-400 Hz, a little more than on the requinto; give way at 2-4 kHz to the lead and the voice | 100-200 Hz body of its bass notes; 8-12 kHz strum air | iZotope; Houghton (cut the secondary source where the primary lives) |

Timbales and congas are kept nearer the centre or spread to avoid clashing with each other; timbales usually get no reverb (Sound On Sound 2006) [S].

**Glue** [H]: both guitars to one short room; percussion together; a gentle mix-bus compressor.

**Where Spectre stands** [M] (session 014's project): nine `acguitar` tracks (requinto variants and segunda variants: articulations, not simultaneous layers), one bass, three `darbuka` tracks standing in for the bongó, a shaker for the maracas, one voice. No instrument reaches the top octave: the master measures 5.5-6 dB under the reference albums' median from 5.3 to 12 kHz and 10 dB under at 12-15.5 kHz, which is as dark as the bolero and acoustic albums and far darker than bachata (next section). If a brief wants the bachata brightness, the missing layer is the top of the percussion (a real güira or maracas sound) and string noise on the guitars.

**How to build it in Spectre** (engine since 2026-11-25; docs/MANUAL.md "Production"). Set `"genre": "latinpop"`: light presets (-1.5 dB cuts, no boosts) for the guitars, the hip hop map for a dembow kit.
- *Guitars*: requinto and segunda on a `guitars` bus with one short `room` return (`mix.returns`, size 0.3, `predelay_ms` 10); the requinto's built-in double is its `chorus` fx, not a layer.
- *Percussion*: bongó and güira or maracas on a `perc` bus (light `comp`), the güira with `"humanize": {"ms": 3, "beta": 1.0}` (a player's drift, not jitter).
- *Soft reggaetón-pop*: the dembow kit and an 808 stack as in docs/HIPHOP.md "How to build it in Spectre".
- *Voice*: `singer` with `double`; its own `hall` return ducked by the voice.

## Granularity: strands, regions and eras (researched 2026-11-25; PRODUCTION.md section 5, item 10)
The sections above cover the *intimate* strands that session 014 needed. Latin pop as a whole is wider: by the Latin Recording Academy's and the charts' use of the word it now includes urbano (reggaetón, Latin trap, dembow), música mexicana (corridos tumbados, sierreño, cumbia norteña) and the tropical styles (salsa, cumbia, merengue), all sung in Spanish and all crossing into each other. This section names the dimensions along which it varies, says where each boundary lies and why, states what holds across all of them, lists each subdivision's elements as checkable items, gives a mastering profile per subdivision, and closes with a current-practice checklist. Labels as above: **[M]** measured here, **[S]** a named source, **[H]** hand-tuned or Spectre's own reading. Artists and records are named as schools and evidence only, never as tracks to copy (standing rule 13).

### A. The dimensions, and why each boundary is where it is
Latin pop varies along three dimensions, and they are not independent: **strand** is the one that changes the most audible elements (rhythm cell, instruments, tempo, low end, top end, loudness); **region** mostly decides which strands exist and adds a local accent to them; **timeline** moves every strand, with a few turns that are dated and measurable. A brief names its strand first, then, if it wants, an era and a region.

**Strand** (the primary dimension). Each boundary is a change of rhythm cell, of the instrument that carries the groove, and of measured tempo or tonal balance (section C):
| Strand | What separates it from its neighbours | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Bachata** (traditional *amargue*; modern; urban/pop) | guitar-led (requinto, segunda), 8th-note pulse with derecho, majao, mambo sections, bongó and güira; tempo about 125-130 | Ubisoft parts 1-3; catalogue [M] |
| **Bolero and balada** (trío bolero, orchestral bolero, balada romántica, the Latin pop ballad, acoustic pop) | slow (75-85), the sway of half-note bass and offbeat chords, no drum kit or a soft one; the bolero and acoustic records have the darkest top end of any strand | Soundbrenner; PBS; catalogue and `latinpop_dark` [M] |
| **Reggaetón and romantic dembow** | the dembow cell (3+3+2 over a four-on-the-floor kick) at 88-100 bpm, a synth or 808 low end, the heaviest sub band | Wikipedia, Reggaeton; Mixgraph [M]; tags [M] |
| **Dominican dembow** | the same cell sped up (about 115-140), stripped down, chanted call-and-response | Wikipedia, Dominican dembow; Berklee 2024; TIDAL |
| **Latin trap** | trap's half-time 808 and hi-hat grid instead of the dembow cell; rapped and sung, Auto-Tuned | Wikipedia, Latin trap |
| **Música mexicana: corridos tumbados, sierreño, cumbia norteña** | acoustic: requinto and 12-string guitars, tololoche or bass, often tuba and charchetas from banda, in a "fast, driving 6/8" (Soundtrap); mostly without a drum kit; or cumbia's two-step with accordion | Wikipedia, Corridos tumbados; Soundtrap; LA Times 2024 |
| **Tropical: salsa, cumbia, merengue, plena** | live Afro-Caribbean percussion sections and horns (salsa: clave, congas, timbales, piano montuno; cumbia: güiro, accordion; merengue: tambora, güira, 2/4 at a fast tempo) | Wikipedia, Clave; LA Times 2024; catalogue [M] |
| **Latin dance-pop** (the 1990s-2000s crossover pop) | pop production with Latin percussion colour; "Latinized dance-pop and adult contemporary balladeering" | Wikipedia, Latin pop |

**Region** (where it applies). Region matters through the strands it produced and the accent it puts on shared ones; no measurement in this pass separated regions inside one strand, so the region notes are [S] only:
- **Dominican Republic:** bachata and Dominican dembow (the faster dembow, linked to the country's "historical preference" for merengue: Wikipedia, Dominican dembow); merengue. Dominican Spanish (s-aspiration) is not modelled by the engine (docs/voice/languages/es.md).
- **Puerto Rico:** reggaetón (from Panama's Spanish reggae and Jamaica's Dem Bow riddim; the 1990s underground of DJ Playero and DJ Nelson), Latin trap, salsa; in 2025 plena, bomba and jíbaro music on the year's most awarded album (DTMF; NPR 2026).
- **Colombia (Medellín):** a "sanded-down, pop-oriented" reggaetón that "filtered Reggaeton and made it radio music" (producer Sky Rompiendo, as quoted in Remezcla and Dazed); cumbia and vallenato roots; guaracha (aleteo) as an electronic offshoot (Wikipedia, Guaracha (Colombia)).
- **Mexico and the US border:** bolero (trova yucateca, the trío romántico), balada, mariachi and banda, sierreño and corridos tumbados (Sonora, Sinaloa, Jalisco and Mexican-American Los Angeles, where Rancho Humilde built the scene: Wikipedia, Corridos tumbados); "reggaetón mexa".
- **US (New York, Miami, Los Angeles):** urban bachata (Aventura and Prince Royce are New Yorkers), Miami as the 1990s factory of the Latin ballad and pop (Wikipedia, Latin ballad), Los Angeles for tumbados.
- **Spain:** the balada romántica of the 1970s-80s (Julio Iglesias, Camilo Sesto, Raphael), flamenco-pop (Rosalía: electronic textures over flamenco's "acoustic guitar and hand percussion" on *El Mal Querer* 2018; a collage of flamenco, bachata, reggaetón, dembow and bolero on *Motomami* 2022; an orchestral record with the London Symphony Orchestra, *Lux*, 2025), and reggaetón as the most-listened genre for years (Wikipedia, Reggaeton).
- **Argentina:** cumbia (villera) and its club offshoot RKT, cuarteto (the most-listened genre in Argentina in 2024), guaracha (the biggest trend of early 2025), and trap (Spotify newsroom 2025; Interspace 2025). Not researched further here: none of Spectre's briefs has asked for it.

**Timeline.** The boundaries are dated events that changed the sound, checked against the catalogue where data exists:
| Turn | What changed | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1987-1992** | bachata goes electric: Blas Durán's "Mujeres hembras" (1987) is the first with electric guitar, with "faster tempi, and multitrack recording"; maracas give way to the güira; *Bachata Rosa* (1990) takes it abroad; Luis Miguel's *Romance* (1991) revives the orchestral bolero | Wikipedia, Bachata (music); Bolero |
| **1990s-2004** | Latin pop as "Latinized dance-pop and adult contemporary balladeering", Miami-produced; ballads 4:00-5:20 long | Wikipedia, Latin pop; Latin ballad; catalogue lengths [M] |
| **2004-2008** | reggaetón's boom ("Gasolina", 2004; by 2008 the "biggest-selling genre of Latin music"); bachatón from 2005; urban bachata (Aventura) | Wikipedia, Reggaeton; Bachata |
| **about 2010** | ballads lose Hispanic radio to "uptempo genres such as bachata, reggaeton, and Spanish-language electropop" | Wikipedia, Latin ballad |
| **2014-2017, the urbano and streaming turn** | the Latin catalogue's 32-97 Hz band rises 3.5 dB, almost all after 2014 [M]; "Despacito" (2017: 89 bpm, a cuatro, güira, guache, timbales over sequenced drums) becomes the most-streamed song in the world and marks a "before and after" | this doc, "Era assessment" [M]; Wikipedia, Despacito |
| **2018** | "a sharper minimalist dembow" with "more syncopated rhythmic experiments" ("Te Boté", "Adictiva"); Latin trap's first Hot 100 number one ("I Like It") | Wikipedia, Reggaeton; Latin trap |
| **2019-2023** | corridos tumbados named (Natanael Cano, 2019) and, in 2023, the first regional Mexican top 10s on the Hot 100 ("Ella Baila Sola", "un x100to"); reggaetón with house and electronic textures ("Dakiti" 2020); *Un Verano Sin Ti* (2022) | Wikipedia; Billboard 2023 |
| **2024-2026, current practice** | a turn back to roots, played by people: salsa streams up more than 140% in five years with listeners 18-24 the second-largest group (Spotify, March 2025); *DTMF* (2025: plena, bomba, salsa, jíbaro beside reggaetón and trap) wins Album of the Year (February 2026); Karol G's *Tropicoqueta* (2025: cumbia, vallenato, merengue, salsa around a reggaetón base); a revival of classic reggaetón's chanting and "dirty synths" among young artists (La Tercera 2026); bolero-pop (Ángela Aguilar's *Bolero*, nominated 2024; Kali Uchis and Peso Pluma's "Igual que un ángel", 2024); the reggaetón tempo median at its highest in 2024 (98 bpm, Mixgraph); the joint Romeo Santos and Prince Royce album (November 2025) fuses bachata with R&B and afrobeat | sources in section E |

The 2014-2017 turn is the only one the open tonal data can see (it ends in 2021-2022); everything after 2022 is from sources, and any tonal claim about it is an extrapolation.

### B. The shared core: what holds across every subdivision
What keeps a track Latin pop whichever strand, region or era the brief names. Each item says how far it holds.
1. **A sung Spanish line is the centre of the record** (shared). Latin pop is "pop music performed in Spanish or Portuguese" (Wikipedia, Latin pop); mastering engineers master these records around the voice ("a lot of it for me is making sure that his vocal sounds perfect": Colin Leonard on mastering Bad Bunny, AJC 2026); "the mid-range of a Latin music master will often be the busiest" (Sage Audio). For Spectre, the voice is an instrument (standing rule 14), so the *role* carries over: a melody that a singer would sing, with the middle (1-4 kHz) left to it.
2. **The middle of the spectrum is the genre; the ends are the strand** (shared, measured). Every strand measured here sits within about 3 dB of `latinpop_ref` from 125 Hz to 5 kHz relative to 1 kHz; the strands differ by 12-20 dB at 31.5-50 Hz (banda and norteño -17.5 dB at 31.5 Hz and -6 at 50, Latin trap +2.5 and +6) and by up to 13 dB at 12.5-16 kHz (boleros -22.5 at 16 kHz, bachata -9.5) [M, section D]. Change the low end and the top to move between strands; leave the middle alone.
3. **Song form in 8-bar sections, three to four and a half minutes** (shared; length varies by about a minute). Medians by strand run from 3:06 (banda and norteño) to 4:00-4:25 (tropical) [M, section C]; urbano singles are shrinking (3:36 in 2015-17, 3:13 in 2021) [M].
4. **A plucked or struck lead instrument that answers the voice** (shared as a role; the instrument varies by strand). The requinto "riffs off the vocal melody" in bachata (Ubisoft part 3), is the lead of the trío romántico (Wikipedia, Requinto guitar; LA Times 2024) and of corridos tumbados ("fast melodies, counter-melodies": Soundtrap); a Puerto Rican cuatro plays the hook of "Despacito" (Wikipedia, Despacito); the accordion in cumbia and norteño (LA Times 2024). The reading "answers, in the gaps" is Spectre's [H], from the call-and-response these sources describe.
5. **Hand or scraped percussion on the subdivision above the beat** (shared except in the guitar-only strands): güira and bongó (bachata), maracas, claves, bongó (bolero; Soundbrenner), timbal and palito in the dembow kit (Wikipedia, Reggaeton), güira and guache synced with the hi-hat in "Despacito", güiro as "the heartbeat of cumbia" (LA Times 2024). Corridos tumbados get their urban feel "almost entirely without digital drum kits" (Soundtrap), though some add trap drums (LA Times 2024): **varies** there.
6. **Mostly major in the romantic strands, mostly minor in urbano** (varies, measured): 15-27% minor in the bachata, bolero, ballad, acoustic and reggaetón-pop albums [M, "Keys"]; 73% minor in reggaetón at large (Mixgraph).
7. **Not a loudness-war genre, as released until 2021** (shared, measured): the whole-track loudness estimates of every strand fall between -9.6 and -12.7 LUFS (section D) [M]; the loud masters of the 2020s are not in the open data.

**Era inside the strands** (where data exists): reggaetón's sub band grew by about 1.5-2 dB from 2004-14 to 2015-21 and its singles shortened by about 20 s after 2017; its tempo moved little until 2021 (93-95) and then rose (Mixgraph: 98 in 2024) [M]. The Latin tags as a whole gained 3.5 dB at 32-97 Hz, almost all after 2014 ("Era assessment" above): most of it is likely the urbano share of the catalogue growing rather than every strand getting heavier: the reggaetón tag itself gained only 1.5-2 dB, while the tags' total rose 3.5 dB [H: a reading of the M rows; the urbano share of the tags was not counted]. Bachata's tag lengths fell from about 4:10 to 3:40 after 2012 [M].

### D. Mastering per subdivision
**What could be measured, and what could not.** The open catalogue gives, per track, a whole-track loudness estimate (ReplayGain, LUFS ≈ -23.7 - RG, ±1-2 dB), a mono Bark-band spectrum and Essentia's dynamic complexity; it gives **no** true peak, no loudness of the loudest section, no PLR or short-term LRA, no stereo width or low-end correlation (AcousticBrainz analysed a mono downmix), and nothing after 2021-2022. No published per-strand measurement of those was found (searched for reggaetón, Latin trap and corrido mastering figures and for measured hits such as "Despacito": only advice and sales pages). **Reading it** [M, with the caveats above]:
- **Loudness is not what separates the strands** in released masters to 2021: everything except the bolero sits at -9.6 to -11.6 LUFS (and the estimate is good to ±1-2 dB). The bolero is 1.5-3 dB quieter. Urbano masters were *not* measurably louder than bachata or banda in this data; Sage's advice that Latin masters go louder "when hip-hop elements are present" is not contradicted for the 2020s (no data) but is not visible before 2022. Streaming normalisation (Spotify -14 LUFS, Apple Music about -16) turns all of them down to the same level anyway, so a louder master gains nothing on playback (Spotify for Artists, "Loudness normalization"; Apple's figure as reported by mastering guides).
- **Dynamics separate the sung-slow strands from the rest:** bolero and balada move most between quiet and loud (4.3-4.4 dB), bachata least (3.0-3.4, as even as trance), urbano and tropical in between. Keep the bolero's and ballad's verse-to-chorus step in the master (the existing `latinpop` setting already limits lightly at -11).
- **Low end separates three families:** urbano (an 808 or synth sub, +5.5 to +6.5 dB at 50-63 Hz re 1 kHz), the band strands (bachata, ballad, tropical: -1 to +2 at 50 Hz), and the acoustic-brass strands (bolero -1.5, banda and norteño -6 at 50 Hz).
- **The top separates bachata (bright) from bolero and acoustic (dark)**; urbano, ballad and tropical sit near the middle, banda and norteño a little dark (3 dB at 6.3-10 kHz).
- **Width and mono point:** no measurement; every strand keeps the genre's mono below 120 Hz (Sage; the engine default) [S]. Not a reason for a strand setting.

**Targets and settings per kind of brief.** A separate target only where the medians differ by more than the data's interquartile spread (about 3.5-4 dB per band in the bass, 2 in the middle): urbano and música mexicana qualify; tropical and the ballad do not (they fit `latinpop_ref`).

### E. Bleeding edge (2024-2026): a checklist
For a brief that asks for "the newest sound" without naming a strand, or names a strand and wants it current. Each row: what, the source, and whether the engine can do it today (docs/MANUAL.md; engine/lang_es.py; docs/voice/VOICES.md "Spanish", "Male voices").
| # | Element | Source | Engine |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Roots played by people beside the beat**: a live-sounding tropical layer (salsa piano montuno and horns, plena and bomba, cumbia's güiro and accordion, bachata guitars) over or instead of a programmed urbano groove; tracks that change genre mid-song (mambo to dembow to merengue) | DTMF (NPR 2026, Hollywood Reporter 2025); *Tropicoqueta* (NPR, Grammy.com 2025); Spotify salsa data (March 2025) | **partly**: guitars, piano, bass, a dembow kit, `strings`; **no** congas, timbales, bongó, güira, güiro, plena hand drums, tambora, accordion or horns (approximations only: "Engine mapping") |
| 2 | **Reggaetón tempo 95-100** (2024 median 98; 2020 92); Dominican dembow 120-140 | Mixgraph 2026 [M]; TIDAL [S] | yes |
| 3 | **Classic dembow sounds back**: raw drums, the old-school "Pounder" kick-snare-timbal palette, chanted hooks (*chanteo*), "dirty synths"; samples of 2000s perreo | La Tercera 2026; SoundCloud 2025; Wikipedia, Reggaeton (riddims) | mostly: `kick`, `snare`, `rim`, `handclap`, `polysynth` with `drive`/`sat`; **no** timbal or timbal roll; chanting only as sung `svs` lines (no rap or speech voice) |
| 4 | **Bachata with R&B, afrobeat and urban beats** under the requinto; Aventura-style "tearful string melodies" | Romeo Santos & Prince Royce 2025 (Metro PR, Rolling Stone en Español); Billboard Español on "La Bachata" (2022) | mostly: `acguitar` (steel) with `chorus`, `ebass`, `strings` (weak), a soft trap or afrobeat kit; **no** electric requinto preset, no real güira |
| 5 | **Bolero-pop**: a bolero's harmony and sway sung by pop and urbano voices, crooned close, sometimes with a modern low end | Ángela Aguilar *Bolero* (Latin Grammy nominations 2024); Kali Uchis & Peso Pluma (2024) | yes: `acguitar`, `piano`, `ebass`, `hanami-es-soft` / `hanami-male-es`; the requinto is a transposed `acguitar` |
| 6 | **Corridos tumbados and sierreño**: requinto runs over a 12-string strum in 6/8 or 3/4, tololoche or tuba bass, charchetas; urban flow, Auto-Tune, no drum kit | Wikipedia; Soundtrap; LA Times 2024 | **partly**: `acguitar` (steel) for requinto and 12-string (no 12-string course doubling; approximate with a second `acguitar` detuned a few cents and an octave layer); **no** tuba, tololoche, charcheta, trombone; compound meter written on the 16-step grid (MANUAL "Other meters") |
| 7 | **Cumbia crossover**: cumbia norteña under a pop or urbano voice ("un x100to", 2023); Argentine cumbia and guaracha | Billboard 2023; Spotify newsroom 2025 | **partly**: güiro as `shaker` (weak), **no** accordion |
| 8 | **Auto-Tune as an instrument** on urbano, trap and tumbado vocals (hard pitch steps, a robotic edge on held notes) | Wikipedia, Latin trap; Corridos tumbados | **no** pitch-correction effect; `svs_vibrato` 0 removes vibrato but not the stepped glide |
| 9 | **The voice as the centre of the master**, a "deep" vocal with a consistent high-frequency texture; masters "as loud as other projects that are out" | Colin Leonard (mastering DTMF; AJC 2026) | yes in method (voice level and EQ in the mix, `latinpop` master); the master cannot single out the voice |
| 10 | **Length about 3:00** for urbano and pop singles (charting songs about 3:00 in 2024); ballads, bachata and tumbados keep 3:30-4:00 | Chartmetric 2024; catalogue lengths [M] (section C) | yes |
| 11 | **Mixed-genre form**: a beat switch or a change of rhythm cell at a section boundary (dembow to house, mambo to merengue) | NPR on *Tropicoqueta*; "Dakiti" (Rolling Stone 2020) | yes: sections with their own patterns, `bpm` per section and tempo ramps (MANUAL "SECTION") |
| 12 | **Tonal target by strand** (section D): `latinpop_urbano` for anything with a dembow or 808 low end, `latinpop_mexicano` for tumbados, the existing three for the rest; every curve is **extrapolated** after 2021 | [M] to 2021 | yes (the two new ones in engine/targets.json since 2026-11-26) |


**Which strand for which brief.** A brief names its strand (and, if it wants, era and region); a brief that names none takes current practice: the strand the feeling calls for ("What Latin pop covers today" above), made with the checklist above and mastered with the strand's target from D. For an intimate subject the recommendation above stands (a bolero-bachata reading); E's rows 1, 4 and 5 are the current forms of it.

### F. Engine mapping for the wider strands: what exists, what is missing
Beyond the gaps already listed in "Engine mapping" above (requinto, bongó, güira, congas, timbales, claves, accordion, tambora, cuatro, brass), the strands in A-E need:
| Need | Engine today | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| Dembow and Latin trap drums, 808 | `kick`, `snare`, `rim`, `handclap`, `hat`, `metalhat`, `shaker`, `808` with glide, `kicksub`/`kickclick` stacks, `clip` on a bus (MANUAL; docs/HIPHOP.md) | **timbal and timbal roll**, palito; the old "Pounder" palette has to be approximated |
| Música mexicana | `acguitar` (steel) for requinto and armonía; `ebass` | **tuba, tololoche** (upright), **charcheta**, trumpet, trombone, saxophone; a **12-string** course doubling (approximate with a second `acguitar` a few cents off and an octave layer, [H]) |
| Tropical | `piano`, `ebass`, `cowbell`, `shaker` | **congas, timbales (paila, cáscara), bongó, güiro, tambora, horn section, accordion** |
| Compound and fast meters | 6/8 and 3/4 on the 12- or 24-step triplet grid; 2/4 as double bars; tempo per section (MANUAL "Drum steps", "Other meters", "SECTION") | none essential |
| Auto-Tune as a sound | `singer` `svs` with `svs_vibrato` 0 (no vibrato) | **no pitch correction or hard-tune effect** (stepped pitch, robotic held notes); proposal: a `tune` fx that snaps the svs pitch curve to the scale with a retune speed |
| Rap, chant, ad-libs | none: `singer` sings notes | **no rhythmic speech voice** for chanteo, rap verses or dembow call-and-response; the svs voice can chant on one pitch with short notes, unmeasured [H] |
| Spanish varieties | Latin American Spanish (seseo, yeísmo) in `engine/lang_es.py`, which fits Mexican, Colombian and most Caribbean pop singing | **Caribbean features** (s-aspiration and final-s loss, Puerto Rican l for r, velar n) and **Castilian distinción** (`th`, in the bank, unused) not modelled (docs/voice/languages/es.md "Still open"); for a Spain brief, write it in Latin American Spanish or flag the gap |
| Voices | `hanami-es`, `hanami-es-soft` (female), `hanami-male-es` (male, dark, soft); Spanish 96% by Whisper (docs/voice/VOICES.md) | no bright male tenor for bachata or reggaetón leads; no baritone with grit for tumbados [H: by the strands' descriptions] |
| Master targets | `latinpop_ref`, `latinpop_dark`, `latinpop_bright` | `latinpop_urbano`, `latinpop_mexicano` (D; in engine/targets.json since 2026-11-26); nothing measured after 2021 for any strand |
