# Session 014: Love letter (Latin pop) — Spectre's journal

## 0. SIT WITH IT

**The feeling.** A love letter is written at a table, late, by one lamp. In the body it is a held breath and a hand that moves, stops, and moves again: warm in the chest, a little afraid in the wrist. In time it is slow and uneven: a sentence runs, halts, is struck through, starts over and gets one word further. In space it is very small: a page, a hand, the distance of a forearm. Nobody else is in the room, and the one it is for is not there either. Tender, not sad; brave in a quiet way.

**What the letter IS in this track.** The act of writing. The requinto is the hand: it writes a phrase in 8th notes, stops short, and **crosses it out** (two dead, palm-muted strokes on the note it just reached, then a hole), then starts the phrase again and gets further. That cross-out is my one detail held closely: it is the only "ugly" sound in the track and the listener learns it in the first ten seconds. The sentence the hand is looking for is the chorus line; it fails to end in chorus 1 (stops on the 9th, struck through), nearly ends in chorus 2 (the 3rd), is said whole and aloud by the guitars in the mambo, and is finally written small and clean, with no cross-outs, in the last chorus. The track ends on the **signature**.
Second mechanism, in voice-leading (010): in the last chorus the voice falls B♭4 to A4 while the guitar climbs D4-E4-G4 to the same A4. Addressed to you: two lines, one note.

**The strand: bolero-bachata, and the two halves mean different things.** The verses are bolero (a fingerpicked nylon guitar in 8ths, bass half-quarter-quarter, maracas): one person alone at the table. The choruses bring the bachata's body in (the segunda's muted upbeat chop, bongó in majao, the bass's pickup into 3): the memory of the other person, the sway of two. The mambo keeps its cultural meaning (the singer stops, the guitars take over, the band's release) but here the release is the confession. Why not the others: a piano ballad swells toward a declaration to a room; acoustic pop would be pleasant and risk the café; a dembow would make it a party. The requinto can hesitate like a hand; nothing else in the kit can.

**Tempo, key, mode.** 84 bpm (bolero median 82), straight 8ths, 4/4. D major: the voice's sweet spot (F♯4-B4) sits on its 3rd, 5th and 6th, the guitars ring on open strings, the bass roots stay on A1 and above. Major, with the borrowed minor iv (Gm6) as the ache under the word "decir", a passing ♯I diminished in the verse, and secondary dominants (B7, D7, F♯7). The only minor stretch is the first half of the mambo (Bm): what was hard to say, before it is said.

**The sounds that hold it.** (1) the requinto: `acguitar`, high, bright, close and dry, with its cross-out strokes; (2) the segunda: nylon guitar, fingerpicked in the verses, chopped in the choruses; (3) one soft voice, `hanami-es-soft`, close to the microphone. Under them a fingered bass, maracas and a low bongó, judged soloed before they stay. No strings, no piano swell.

**The voice (an instrument: a call the requinto answers, a refrain fragment, a sign-off).** `hanami-es-soft`, my own words:
- verse 1: "te escribo" (I'm writing to you) — the call that starts the hand; verse 2: "te escribo despacio" (I'm writing to you slowly)
- choruses: "lo que no supe decir" (what I never knew how to say) — "decir" lands on B♭4 over Gm6; the requinto answers and tries to finish the sentence. What was never said is not sung: it stays in the guitar, between two people.
- the end: "siempre tuya" (always yours) — the signature.
Nothing in the mambo (the singer stops) or in verse 3.

**Nearest wrong feelings and what separates them.**
- Club reggaetón banger: no dembow, no kick at all, no 808; the pulse is fingers and maracas.
- Wedding-band ballad: no strings, no key change, no cliché fills; the last chorus shrinks instead of asking for applause.
- Tourist postcard: no flamenco rasgueo, no Phrygian flourish; runs in thirds and sixths only at phrase ends; the güira stand-in stays out unless it convinces soloed.
- Melodrama: the loudest moment is a band playing a bit harder at 84 bpm, in the middle; the ending is a whisper. Tenderness, not tragedy: major key.
- Generic radio love song: the cross-out, the sentence that needs four tries, the diminished passing chord and the minor iv are specific; no I-V-vi-IV.
- Background music: the holes after each cross-out ask for attention; the phrase is visibly unfinished, so the listener waits for it.

**The arc across about 3:30 (72 bars at 2.86 s).**
mid-sentence opening (4) · verse 1, bolero, hesitant (8) · chorus 1, majao, sentence fails (8) · verse 2, derecho, fewer cross-outs (8) · chorus 2, sentence nearly (8) · mambo A in Bm, the hand stops stopping (8) · mambo B, the sentence said whole, the peak (8) · verse 3, read back, an octave lower, no cross-outs (8) · last chorus, the smallest: voice and one guitar meet on A4 (8) · signature (4).

**BREAK YOUR OWN HABITS.** Read: 008 (Anyway), 009 (Here You Are), 010 (Unison), 012 (The Note She Kept), and the others' arrangements in LESSONS. What I keep doing in song form: the intro is the main figure alone (008, 009); a bridge where the band leaves and the drums come back one by one (008, 009); a beat of silence before the last chorus (008, 009, 007); the last chorus is the biggest, lead in unison, top note held (008, 009, 010's peak); voices join in steps as "company" (008, 009, 012); the key lift as a door (012); an outro where every motif passes together (008, 009); a coda with the figure alone, unresolved or breaking off (008, 009).
**Departures, chosen for this feeling:**
1. **It starts mid-sentence.** No lone figure: bar 1 is the whole small band already inside a phrase, on the ii chord, as if the page is half written. A letter you open is already going.
2. **The loudest moment is the middle, and it is the confession, not a party**: the mambo at 1:43-2:28. The band doesn't leave in the "bridge"; it is the only place it plays out.
3. **The last chorus is the smallest** (voice, one guitar, no percussion, no bass pickup), with no silence before it: the mambo thins straight into the read-back verse. What a letter says, it says quietly.
4. **It ends on the signature and it closes**: "siempre tuya" over iv-I, then a three-note sign-off and one chord. Not a figure breaking off: a letter is finished, folded and sent. What stays open is only that the voice lands on the 3rd, not the root: sent, not answered.
One voice only, no company in steps; no key change. (No outside research this time: the form bends come from the subject.)
