# 012 Reunion: journal

## 0. SIT WITH IT

**The feeling.** Somebody comes back to a door after years of not speaking. Not a holiday visit: a return nobody was sure would happen. In the body it is the held breath on the step, cold hands, a throat that is not sure it will carry, and then the chest letting go when a voice inside the house picks up the tune. In time it is hesitant, then even: the one outside sings a little slow and leaves room; once they are together the carol walks at its own pace. In space it is a dry, close street (snow, no tail) and then a warm parlour with a reed organ in it. The feeling I commit to: **relief that still has the years in it**. Warm, steady, not jubilant; forgiven, not festive.

**What reunion IS in sound.** One carol verse broken across a door. The tune is "It Came Upon the Midnight Clear" (the American one), whose verse is a question (lines 1-2, ending on the dominant), an answer (lines 3-4, home), then "Peace on the earth, good will to men" (lines 5-6), then the answer again (7-8). Outside, one voice sings the question alone in A-flat. Under line 3 the house starts to sound through the door (the organ, muffled), and line 4, the phrase that brings the tune home, is sung from inside. The door opens on a held chord in which one inner note rises a semitone (A-flat becomes A natural: A-flat major turns into F7) and the second half of the same verse is sung in B-flat, in the room. The meeting itself is in the voice-leading: the one who stayed holds a single D4 through "Peace on the earth, good will to men"; the one who left starts the line an octave above (D5) and the tune itself falls the octave onto that held note. The carol was written with the reunion in it; I only have to give the two notes to two people.

**Nearest wrong feelings.**
- Greeting card: sparkle and smile. Separated by: one bell, not a shimmer; the street section dry and bare; the meeting is the quietest spot; no musicbox, no sleigh anything.
- Mall jingle: the tune as a brand over a pad. Separated by: the tune is never looped whole at the start; it is broken, carried by two people, and the first complete verse comes only after the door.
- Sad ballad: a lonely voice as the subject. This is my nearest danger in the street section. Separated by: major key, a walking 6/8, the voice alone for only four carol bars before the house answers; no piano.
- Church service: organ plus four parts from first verse to last. The danger of verses 2 and 3. Separated by: a parlour-sized room, soft organ, upper voices only, bells from the street, and a form that is a story, not a liturgy; no cathedral tail.
- Novelty: nothing here winks.

**Quote.** "It Came Upon the Midnight Clear", tune CAROL (R. S. Willis 1850), words E. H. Sears 1849; notes copied from the verified `carol_pickup` and `carol` blocks of docs/CAROLING.md (source cited there: Hutchins, *Carols Old and Carols New*, Boston 1916, carol 607, checked against hymnary.org and Mutopia). All eight lines by phrase table ranges; A-flat (`transpose` -2) for lines 1-4 of verse 1, B-flat (as verified) for everything after. Words sung: only line 5 "Peace on the earth, good will to men" and line 8 "to hear the angels sing" (stanza 1 underlay from the phrase table); everything else on "ooh"/"ah". Harmony is my own (Bb | Eb, C7 to F at the half close, D to Gm to C7 to F under "Peace on the earth", a cadential 6-4 before the last line).

**Tempo, meter, key.** 6/8 on the 12-step grid. Street: engine bpm 76 (dotted quarter about 51), hesitant. From the door on: 84 (dotted quarter 56). A-flat major, then B-flat major.

**The sounds that hold it.** (1) One female voice, dry and close (hanami), then a second, darker one (hanami-dark) and a soft third (hanami-soft). (2) A reed-organ-like `organ`, first behind a low-pass (the house heard through the door), then open and soft. (3) `bell` at ratio 3.0 as handbells: one bell in the street, a peal once they sing together. Low strings give the organ a pedal. No brass in the engine: verse 2's corner chorale goes to organ plus low strings, where I would have used a Salvation Army quartet (cornet, alto horn, trombone, tuba).

**Space.** Street: dry, short early reflections, almost no tail. Room: one shared reverb, parlour-sized, for all singers. The change happens once, at the door, and means the door.

**The arc.** Bell (street) - question alone - answer through the door - door - the meeting on one note, quiet - home - a verse where the house plays and the voices answer - a full verse, a cappella at "Peace on the earth", full close, the first bell again, no longer alone.

**My habits (from the READMEs of 001-011).** Intros that start from one element and add; a crowd or second voice that joins in steps so the last hook is the biggest (006, 008, 009); a beat or bar of silence before the slam (007, 008, 009, 011); a low-point section about two thirds through (005 "cost", 006 "hour", 008 bridge) followed by the biggest hook; call and response (005, 006); wind-down outros (005, 006; broken in 010 and 011); a key or register lift saved for the end.

**Departures.**
1. The centre of gravity is early and quiet: the meeting falls at about a third of the track and is its most exposed moment (two voices, one held note), not a peak built toward. What follows is consequence, not build. No valley at two thirds.
2. The key lift is not a last-chorus device: it happens once, inside verse 1, between line 4 and line 5, and it is the door.
3. No silence before the arrival: the join is made by a held common tone and one inner voice moving a semitone, the opposite of my cut-then-slam.
I keep call and response on purpose (it is what a door is), and I keep a full last verse because the genre asks for it; its "Peace on the earth" goes a cappella so the fullness has somewhere to come from.
